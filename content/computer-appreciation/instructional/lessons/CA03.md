## Annotated copy-down demonstration
Use a fictional supply exercise with a shared multiplier. Enter B2=4, B3=6 and F1=3. In D2 enter =B2*$F$1. D2 should be 12. Copy the formula one row down into D3, then inspect **the formula bar**, not just the answer.

| Destination | Formula | Annotation | Expected value |
|---|---|---|---:|
| D2 | =B2*$F$1 | Row-specific B2 times fixed F1 | 12 |
| D3 | =B3*$F$1 | B moves down one row; F1 stays fixed | 18 |

The two formulas have the same structure but deliberately read different quantities. Now change F1 to 4. The predicted results are 16 and 24. This is why a single shared input is useful: both formulas refer to the same parameter without manually editing both calculations.
The previously executed alternative-engine check verified the 12/18 arithmetic, not the act of dragging, pressing Ctrl+D or cycling F4 in Excel.

## Diagnose a copied formula in stages
Suppose D3 contains =B3*F2 and gives an unexpected result. First compare its references with the intended dependencies. B3 is appropriate for the new row, but F2 is a shifted parameter; merely formatting D3 or retyping the displayed answer hides the broken relationship.
Correct the original formula's shared reference, repeat the copy into the intended destination and inspect both formulas. If the answer remains unexpected, check the stored inputs and calculation settings rather than assuming every error is caused by dollar signs.
For a mixed reference such as $B2, only the column is fixed. Moving one column right and one row down produces $B3. By contrast, B$2 becomes C$2. Read each coordinate separately instead of treating any dollar sign as “freeze everything”.

## Troubleshooting case with reasoning
An exercise asks for the mean of 6 and 10. A learner enters =6+10/2 and obtains 11. The software has followed evaluation order: 10/2=5, then 6+5=11. The intended mean is =(6+10)/2=8. Parentheses express the intended grouping; changing a number format cannot turn the wrong formula into the right calculation.
Finally, distinguish an ordinary reference to another sheet from a formula that refers back to its own output. A circular dependency concerns the path of calculation, not the presence of an exclamation mark or dollar sign.
