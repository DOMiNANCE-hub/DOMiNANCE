# Describing and counting numeric data

## Learning goals
Distinguish totals, averages and counts, and interpret simple descriptive statistics.

## Choose the question before the function
Suppose a fictional practice dataset contains 2, 4 and 9. SUM gives 15; AVERAGE gives an arithmetic mean of 5. Neither is a count: there are three observations. For 2, 4 and 9 the median is 4, the middle value after ordering. A mode describes a most frequent value; the set 2, 2, 9 has mode 2. A mean and median answer different questions even when they sometimes happen to match.

## Count what you intend to count
COUNT counts numeric entries in a referenced range. COUNTA counts nonempty entries, including text. If A2:A5 contains numeric 10, numeric 12, text “pending” and one genuinely empty cell, COUNT(A2:A5) gives 2 and COUNTA(A2:A5) gives 3. A text label is evidence of an entry, not a numeric measurement.
COUNTIF applies one criterion. For numeric values 35, 40, 55 and 40 in B2:B5, =COUNTIF(B2:B5,">=40") gives 3. The comparison operator is part of the quoted criterion. State the boundary explicitly: >=40 includes 40; >40 excludes it.

## Spread and selection
MIN and MAX return the smallest and largest numeric values. SMALL and LARGE select the k-th smallest or largest. The lecturer notes distinguish sample standard deviation, STDEV, from whole-population standard deviation, STDEVP. Choose the population or sample interpretation from the study design; a spreadsheet does not make that decision for you. These legacy function names appear in the supplied notes.

## Try it
For 2, 4 and 9, calculate the total, mean, median and count by hand. Then add the text label “pending” in another cell and explain why it should not be interpreted as a fourth numeric observation.
