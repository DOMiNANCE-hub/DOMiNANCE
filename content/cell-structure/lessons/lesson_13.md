# Lesson 13 — Why Specimens Must Be Prepared

### The preparation sequence

Cells and tissues cannot always be examined directly. Some are too thick for light or electron transmission, and transparent structures may lack sufficient contrast. Preparing a specimen helps preserve its structure, provide mechanical support, produce suitably thin sections and make important components visible.

The main procedures are **fixation → embedding → sectioning → staining**. **Dehydration** and **clearing** are essential steps within many embedding workflows.

- **Fixation:** rapidly kills and preserves tissue, reducing breakdown and changes in structure.
- **Dehydration:** removes water, commonly using progressively stronger alcohol solutions.
- **Embedding:** surrounds or infiltrates tissue with a supporting material, such as paraffin wax or epoxy resin.
- **Sectioning:** cuts thin slices so light or electrons can pass through appropriately.
- **Staining:** increases contrast between structures that would otherwise look similar.

### Why thickness matters

For **transmission electron microscopy (TEM)**, electrons must pass through the section. Excessively thick tissue scatters electrons and obscures detail. Thin sections are therefore essential. The sectioning range given for electron microscopy is **50–100 nm**, with **approximately 50 nm** also highlighted.

**Concept Connections:** Preservation (fixation) prevents tissue deterioration; support (embedding) makes thin cutting possible; thinness (sectioning) allows transmission; contrast (staining) makes components distinguishable. These stages solve different problems and work together.

**Slide Essentials:** Four named procedures: fixation, embedding, sectioning, staining. Dehydration precedes embedding. TEM needs ultrathin sections because the electron beam must pass through the specimen.

**Optional check:** Why can't an ordinary thick tissue block be placed directly in a TEM? *Too many electrons are scattered, reducing transmission and image detail.*
