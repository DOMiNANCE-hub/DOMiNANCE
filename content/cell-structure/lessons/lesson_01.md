# Lesson 1 — Why Microscopy Matters

### Microscopy and the limits of the eye
The unaided human eye can distinguish objects approximately **0.1 mm (100 µm)** apart under suitable conditions. Many cells and their internal structures are smaller than this, so microscopes are needed to study them. Cells may also be nearly transparent, making **contrast** important for seeing their boundaries and components.

Three ideas are essential:
- **Magnification** makes an object appear larger.
- **Resolution (resolving power)** is the ability to distinguish two closely spaced points as separate.
- **Contrast** is the difference in appearance between a structure and its surroundings, making it easier to see.

A highly magnified image is not necessarily a clear image: magnification without adequate resolution may only enlarge blur.

### Units used in microscopy
| Unit | Symbol | Metres | Useful relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Millimetre | mm | 10⁻³ m | 1 mm = 1,000 µm |
| Micrometre (micron) | µm | 10⁻⁶ m | 1 µm = 1,000 nm |
| Nanometre | nm | 10⁻⁹ m | 1 nm = 10 Å |
| Angstrom | Å | 10⁻¹⁰ m | 10 Å = 1 nm |

**Older notation:** **mµ** (millimicron) is an older name for **nm**. Thus 1,000 mµ = 1 µm and 10⁶ mµ = 1 mm.

### Slide Essentials
- Approximate unaided-eye resolving limit: **0.1 mm = 100 µm**.
- Microscopy requires high **resolving power** and sufficient **contrast**, especially for transparent cells.
- **1 mm = 1,000 µm = 10⁶ nm**; **1 µm = 1,000 nm**; **1 nm = 10 Å**.

### Concept Connections
**Small cell structures → appropriate units → microscopy:** Cell dimensions are commonly expressed in micrometres; much smaller structures may be expressed in nanometres. **Magnification + resolution + contrast → useful image:** All three contribute to what can actually be seen. Later lessons explain how microscope lenses affect image quality and resolution.

**Optional check:** Convert 0.1 mm into micrometres. **Answer:** 100 µm.
