# OR05 — Nucleus: Envelope, Pores and Genetic Instructions

**Source:** BIOL 151 Cell Structure, PDF pages 227–234.

**Learning Mission:** Explain the defining structure, major functions, and key distinctions in this lesson.

## The nuclear envelope
The **nucleus** is a major compartment of eukaryotic cells containing most of their DNA. Its **nuclear envelope** has **two membranes** separated by a **perinuclear space**. The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with the **rough endoplasmic reticulum**. **Nuclear pore complexes** span the envelope and regulate exchange between nucleoplasm and cytoplasm.

The lecture describes an **eightfold-symmetric** protein arrangement surrounding nuclear pores, and uses the term *porosome* for the pore and annulus. **Terminology clarification:** *nuclear pore complex (NPC)* is the standard modern term; *porosome* is also used for a different secretory structure in some literature, so avoid treating it as an unambiguous synonym.

## Selective nuclear transport
The pore does not simply act as a hole that lets all small molecules pass and blocks all large ones. Small molecules may diffuse, while larger cargo can be transported selectively using nuclear transport receptors and targeting signals. Proteins destined for the nucleus are generally synthesised on cytosolic ribosomes and carry **nuclear localisation signals**.

## DNA, chromosomes and chromatin
The slide states **46 chromosomes** in human cells. More precisely, **typical human diploid somatic cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs)**; gametes have 23, and some specialised cells differ. **Chromatin** is DNA associated with proteins, including **histones**. A **nucleosome** is a repeating structural unit of chromatin, consisting of DNA wrapped around a histone core. During cell division, chromatin becomes highly condensed into recognisable chromosomes.

## From gene to protein
**Transcription** copies genetic information from DNA into RNA, including messenger RNA (**mRNA**). **Translation** reads mRNA at ribosomes to assemble amino acids into a protein. A gene does not always encode a protein; some genes encode functional RNAs. The slide's DNA → mRNA → protein sequence remains the central example.

## Concept Connections
**Nucleoplasm** is the material within the nucleus; **chromatin** is DNA plus associated proteins; **histone** helps package DNA; **nucleosome** is the chromatin repeat unit; **transcription** makes RNA; **translation** makes protein.
