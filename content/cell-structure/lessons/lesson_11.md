# Lesson 11 — Electron Microscopy and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

### Why electrons?

Electrons have both particle-like and wave-like properties. When accelerated through a voltage, they have a very short wavelength. This gives electron microscopy the potential to resolve details far smaller than those normally visible with a light microscope.

**Slide formula:** Electron wavelength is presented as **λ ≈ √(1.5/V) nm**, where **V** is the accelerating voltage in volts. For **60 kV**, the slide gives a wavelength of approximately **0.005 nm**.

**Important clarification:** This is an approximate relationship; the extremely short electron wavelength does not by itself guarantee equally small practical resolution. Lens imperfections, specimen contrast and preparation quality also matter.

Two major types of electron microscope are **transmission electron microscopes (TEM)** and **scanning electron microscopes (SEM)**.

### How TEM works

A **transmission electron microscope** passes a focused electron beam through an **ultrathin section** of a specially prepared specimen.

- An **electron gun** produces electrons; a heated cathode and an anode are part of the system described here.
- The electrons are accelerated by a high voltage; the slide gives **50–150 kV** as an example range.
- **Electromagnetic lenses** (coils producing magnetic fields) focus the beam.
- A **condenser lens** focuses electrons towards the specimen.
- Electrons interact with the specimen; different regions scatter electrons differently.
- An **objective lens** and further **projector lenses** form and enlarge the image.
- The image may be viewed on a fluorescent screen or recorded.

### Specimen contrast and practical resolution

Biological specimens contain many low-atomic-number elements such as **carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen**, so they naturally produce relatively weak electron contrast. Preparation techniques strongly influence the final image.

The slides quote **0.2–0.3 nm** resolution for certain electron microscopes and **approximately 300,000×** as a useful magnification figure. Treat these as lecture-specific examples, not universal specifications for every TEM.

### Slide Essentials
- Electrons have wave properties; electron wavelength decreases with increased accelerating voltage.
- Slide formula: **λ ≈ √(1.5/V) nm**; **60 kV → about 0.005 nm**.
- Electron microscopes: **TEM** and **SEM**.
- **TEM** sends electrons through an **ultrathin specimen**.
- Electron gun, cathode/anode, condenser, electromagnetic objective and projector lenses are key components.
- Biological material gives weak contrast because it largely contains low-atomic-number elements.
- Resolution depends on lens quality, contrast and specimen preparation, not wavelength alone.

### Concept Connections
**Accelerating voltage → electron wavelength.** **Electron wavelength → potential resolution.** **Specimen thickness → electron transmission.** **Atomic composition → electron scattering and image contrast.** **Preparation quality → practical image detail.**

**Optional check:** Why must a TEM specimen be ultrathin? *Electrons need to pass through it to form a transmission image.*
