# OR07 — Centrosome and Centrioles

**Source:** BIOL 151 Cell Structure, PDF pages 241–244.

**Learning Mission:** Explain the defining structure, major functions, and key distinctions in this lesson.

## Centrosome: the microtubule-organising centre
In many animal cells, the **centrosome** lies near the nucleus and serves as a principal **microtubule-organising centre (MTOC)**. Microtubules radiate from the surrounding **pericentriolar material (PCM)**, which contains proteins needed to nucleate and anchor microtubules.

## Centrioles
A typical animal-cell centrosome contains **two centrioles**, often arranged approximately **at right angles**. Each centriole has **nine sets of microtubule triplets** arranged in a ring (**9 × 3**). **Do not confuse** this with the **9 + 2** arrangement of microtubule doublets in many motile cilia and flagella.

## Polarity and dynamic growth
A microtubule has a **minus end** and a **plus end**. In the radial arrays described by the lecture, the minus end is usually anchored near the centrosome, while the plus end extends toward the cell periphery and can rapidly grow or shrink. This behaviour is called **dynamic instability**.

## Cell division and cilia
Centrosomes contribute to organisation of the **mitotic spindle**, which separates chromosomes during cell division. Centrioles duplicate once per cell cycle under normal regulation. Centrioles can also give rise to **basal bodies**, which organise cilia and flagella. **Clarification:** not all eukaryotes possess centrioles, and cells can organise microtubules without a canonical centrosome.

## Slide Essentials
Near nucleus; pericentriolar material; two centrioles at ~90°; nine triplets per centriole; microtubule plus end outward; mitotic spindle; centriole duplication; basal bodies and cilia/flagella.

## Concept Connections
**MTOC** organises microtubules; **PCM** surrounds centrioles and nucleates microtubules; **microtubule polarity** distinguishes plus/minus ends; **dynamic instability** is alternating growth/shrinkage; **basal body** anchors and organises a cilium.
