# Lesson 3 — Optical Aberrations

### What are optical aberrations?
**Optical aberrations** are lens defects that reduce image quality. Two major defects of simple microscopes are **spherical aberration** and **chromatic aberration**.

### Spherical aberration
**Spherical aberration** produces blurred images because light rays from a specimen do not all come to the same focus. In a spherical lens, rays near the edge (*peripheral rays*) and rays near the centre are refracted differently, so they may focus at different points. This reduces sharpness and can make fine details harder to distinguish.

Differences in refractive index between materials along the light path, such as glass and air, can also contribute to focusing problems. **Refractive index** describes how strongly light is slowed and bent by a material.

**Ways to reduce spherical aberration:**
1. **Coat or block the periphery of the lens with opaque material** to restrict peripheral rays.
2. **Use suitable lens shapes, including plano-convex lenses** (one flat surface and one curved surface).
3. **Use an oil-immersion lens** to reduce changes in refraction between the specimen and the objective lens.

An **aspherical lens** (with a specially shaped, non-spherical surface) is another method of bringing rays closer to a common focus.

**Understanding note — immersion oil:** The **objective lens** is the microscope lens nearest the specimen. In a typical covered slide, immersion oil fills the gap between the objective lens and the coverslip. Because the oil bends light similarly to glass, it helps reduce unwanted refraction. It does **not** guarantee complete elimination of every cause of spherical aberration.

**Understanding note — lens shape:** A plano-convex lens can reduce spherical aberration in suitable arrangements; it does not automatically remove the defect in every setup.

### Chromatic aberration
**Chromatic aberration**, also called **colour fringing** or **purple fringing**, occurs when a lens focuses different wavelengths (colours) of light at different positions. It results from **dispersion**: the refractive index of glass varies with wavelength.

The image may become blurred or develop coloured edges, including red, green, blue, yellow, purple or magenta fringes.

**Ways to correct chromatic aberration:**
1. **Combine crown glass and flint glass in an achromatic doublet.** Their different optical properties help bring selected colours to approximately the same focus.
2. **Use more complex combinations of lenses** to improve correction.

**Understanding note:** The slide describes crown and flint glass as having equal but opposite focal lengths. In practice, an achromatic doublet is designed by balancing the optical powers and dispersions of its elements; the simple wording should not be taken as a universal construction rule.

### Spherical versus chromatic aberration
| | Spherical aberration | Chromatic aberration |
|---|---|---|
| Main issue | Rays passing through different lens regions focus differently | Different colours focus differently |
| Visible effect | Blur and reduced sharpness | Blur and coloured fringes |
| Typical remedies | Restrict peripheral rays; suitable lens shapes; immersion techniques | Achromatic doublets; more complex corrected lens systems |

### Slide Essentials
- **Two inherent optical defects:** spherical and chromatic aberration.
- **Spherical:** rays fail to meet at a simultaneous focus; image becomes blurred.
- **Spherical correction list:** opaque peripheral coating; plano-convex lenses; oil immersion. Also understand the role of aspherical lenses.
- **Chromatic:** dispersion causes different colours to focus differently; also called colour/purple fringing.
- **Chromatic correction list:** crown + flint glass (**achromatic doublet**); more complex lens systems.

### Concept Connections
**Refraction → focusing:** Lens surfaces bend light to form images. **Lens region → spherical aberration:** Central and peripheral rays may focus differently. **Wavelength → dispersion → chromatic aberration:** Different colours may focus at different positions. **Corrected lenses → clearer microscopy:** Lens design affects how much detail remains visible. **Immersion oil → refractive-index continuity:** A properly matched oil reduces unwanted bending at the glass–oil interface, particularly with a compatible objective lens.

**Optional checks:**
1. Which defect causes coloured fringes? **Chromatic aberration.**
2. Name the two glasses in an achromatic doublet. **Crown and flint glass.**
3. State the three spherical-aberration correction methods listed above. **Opaque peripheral coating, suitable plano-convex lenses, and oil immersion.**
