# Lesson 9 — Ultraviolet Microscopy

### Principle

**Ultraviolet (UV) microscopy** uses ultraviolet light, which has a shorter wavelength than visible light. Shorter wavelengths can improve resolution; an improvement of **approximately twofold** is sometimes described compared with white-light microscopy.

Some specimens absorb UV light naturally. Other specimens can be treated with dyes that absorb UV and emit light at a longer wavelength. This light emission is closely connected to **fluorescence microscopy**, covered next.

### Disadvantages

1. **Ordinary glass lenses absorb much UV light**, so suitable UV-transmitting optical materials, such as quartz, may be needed; these are expensive.
2. **UV light is invisible to the human eye**, so the UV image cannot simply be viewed directly in the usual way.
3. **UV exposure can damage the eyes**, requiring protective optical arrangements, cameras or a suitable viewing screen.

### Slide Essentials
- UV has a shorter wavelength than visible light and may offer roughly twice the resolution in the comparison presented.
- Quartz optics may be required instead of ordinary glass.
- UV cannot be seen directly and can harm the eyes; indirect imaging is necessary.

### Concept Connections
**Wavelength → resolution:** shorter wavelength can reveal finer detail. **UV excitation → fluorescence:** some molecules emit longer-wavelength light after absorbing UV. **Optical material → transmission:** the lens must transmit the wavelengths being used.

**Optional check:** Why are quartz lenses used in some UV microscopes? *Quartz can transmit UV wavelengths that ordinary glass absorbs.*
