# BI04 — Paper chromatography: setup and migration
**Source:** BIOL 151 lecture PDF pp. 392–401. **Status:** editorial review edition; figure and full slide audit outstanding.

## Core lesson

### Preparing the paper
Apply a small concentrated sample spot on a pencil baseline, then place the paper in a developing chamber with the solvent level below the baseline. If the sample is submerged, it may wash directly into the solvent reservoir.

### Development
Solvent travels through paper by capillary action. Different sample components move different distances because their interactions with the mobile and stationary phases differ.

### Recording a chromatogram
Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the end, immediately mark the solvent front, dry it and locate the separated spots. Use suitable visualization for colourless compounds.

### Interpretation limits
A single solvent system may fail to resolve two substances. A spot at the same height as a standard suggests similarity under those conditions, not conclusive chemical identity.

### Lecturer-specific practical details (PDF pp. 392–399)
The lecturer places a **small concentrated sample spot approximately 2 cm from the base** of the chromatography paper, often using a **capillary tube**. The developing solvent (examples: **ethanol, water or acetone**) must remain **below the spot**. Solvent travels through the paper by **capillary action**. Differences in solubility and attraction to the paper's cellulose affect migration. The lecture notes that development can take **minutes to hours**.

If two substances give the same Rf value in one solvent, the lecturer describes **two-way/two-dimensional chromatography**: turn the chromatogram **90°** and develop in a different solvent to improve separation. The lecture also notes that paper chromatography has largely been superseded in many applications by **thin-layer chromatography (TLC)**.

**Understanding note:** Substances that bind irreversibly to the paper may not separate or be recovered appropriately using this method.

## Slide Essentials

## Concept Connections
These concepts connect cell preparation, separation and biochemical interpretation: sample preparation affects which components survive, separation enriches material, and subsequent assays determine what is actually present.
