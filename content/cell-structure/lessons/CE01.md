# CE01 — Cell Disruption and Homogenisation
**Lecture source:** BIOL 151 PDF pages 322–340 (printed slide numbers 329–347). **Academic status:** corrected against extractable slide text; image-only slide verification remains open.

## Cell fractionation: purpose and sequence
Cell fractionation investigates functions of individual cell compartments by disrupting cells and separating their components. The lecturer presents three steps: **(1) disruption or homogenisation**, normally in a supportive **iso-osmotic medium**, producing a **homogenate**; **(2) separation of cell fragments and organelles** for individual study; and **(3) biochemical analysis** of the separated fractions. An iso-osmotic medium helps preserve the structure of organelles, although lysis methods may intentionally use hypotonic conditions.

## Cell homogenisation and lysis
Cell lysis is an early step in cell fractionation and protein purification. The lecture distinguishes physical methods from detergent-based disruption. Choose the method according to the cell type, volume and whether intact organelles or soluble contents are required.

### Mechanical disruption — Waring blender and Polytron (p.324)
Rotating blades grind and disperse relatively large amounts of complex tissues such as liver and muscle. The **Waring blender** resembles a household blender. The **Polytron** draws tissue into a long shaft containing rotating blades. These are not interchangeable with the gentle methods used to isolate fragile organelles.

### Liquid homogenisation — Dounce, Potter–Elvehjem and French press (pp.325–330)
Liquid homogenisation forces a suspension through a narrow gap, shearing membranes. The **Dounce homogeniser** has a rounded glass pestle moved manually inside a glass tube. The **Potter–Elvehjem homogeniser** is another pestle-and-tube design. **Number and speed of strokes** affect disruption efficiency. A **French press** uses a piston to apply high pressure and force material through a small opening. The lecturer specifies **40–250 mL** sample volumes and states that **two passes** are generally sufficient in the example discussed. These are slide-specific descriptions, not universal specifications for every French press.

### Sonication (pp.331–334)
A vibrating probe delivers pulsed, high-frequency sound waves to the suspension. Cavitation involves microscopic vapour bubbles forming and collapsing, generating disruptive forces. To limit heat damage, the lecturer recommends **multiple short bursts with the sample in an ice bath**. The lecture describes sonication as best suited to volumes **below 100 mL**; this is a teaching guideline, not an absolute instrument limit.

### Freeze–thaw (pp.335–336)
Cells are frozen (for example, in a dry-ice/ethanol bath or freezer) and thawed at room temperature or **37 °C**. Ice formation and associated physical stress disrupt membranes. **Repeated cycles** are generally needed. The lecture describes uses in bacterial and mammalian cells, including recovery of bacterial cytoplasmic recombinant proteins. Scientific clarification: ice-crystal injury and osmotic/mechanical stresses contribute; the process is not simply cells swelling because ice expands.

### Mortar and pestle (p.337)
For plant cells, tissue may be frozen in **liquid nitrogen** and manually ground. Tough cell walls containing **cellulose and other polysaccharides** make this especially useful for accessing plant proteins and DNA.

## Additives and facilitators (pp.338–339)
- **Hypotonic buffer:** promotes swelling and easier disruption, but differs from the iso-osmotic medium used to protect isolated organelles.
- **Cell-wall digestion:** the lecturer specifies **200 µg/mL** for an agent used to digest polysaccharide components of yeast and bacterial walls; the extracted slide text does **not identify the agent**, so its name must not be guessed from this source.
- **Glass beads:** can assist mechanical disruption.
- **Nucleases:** nucleic-acid release raises lysate viscosity. The lecture lists **DNase 25–50 µg/mL** and **RNase 50 µg/mL** to reduce viscosity. It notes that nuclease treatment may not be needed after sonication because sonication can shear chromosomes; this depends on protocol.
- **Protease inhibitors:** help limit proteolytic degradation during sample handling.

## Concept Connections
A disruption method changes which cellular structures remain intact. Iso-osmotic support helps preserve organelles for fractionation; hypotonic lysis or detergents can deliberately destroy membranes. Subsequent centrifugation enriches components, and biochemical assays help identify what is in each fraction.

## Short concept checks
1. Name the three stages of cell fractionation. **Disruption, separation, biochemical analysis.**
2. Distinguish the Waring blender from the Polytron. **Both use rotating blades; the Polytron draws tissue into a bladed shaft.**
3. Which two variables affect Dounce/Potter homogenisation? **Stroke number and speed.**
4. Why use short sonication bursts on ice? **To reduce excessive sample heating.**
5. Which sample preparation is used before mortar-and-pestle grinding of plant tissue? **Freezing in liquid nitrogen.**
6. Why can DNase and RNase be added? **To reduce viscosity from released nucleic acids.**
