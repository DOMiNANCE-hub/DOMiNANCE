# Surface-Area-to-Volume Ratio and Cell Size

**Learning mission:** Calculate surface area and volume of cubic models and explain why cell size is constrained.

## 1. The geometric principle
For a cube with side length **L**:

- **Surface area = 6L²**
- **Volume = L³**
- **Surface-area-to-volume ratio = 6/L**, with units of inverse length (e.g. cm⁻¹).

The ratio falls as the cube becomes larger. Surface area grows with the **square** of length, while volume grows with the **cube** of length.

## 2. The lecture's worked examples (PDF p. 202)

| Side length | Area of one face | Volume | Total surface area | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 cm | 1 cm² | 1 cm³ | 6 cm² | 6 cm⁻¹ |
| 2 cm | 4 cm² | 8 cm³ | 24 cm² | 3 cm⁻¹ |
| 4 cm | 16 cm² | 64 cm³ | 96 cm² | 1.5 cm⁻¹ |

**Example:** Doubling the cube's side from 1 cm to 2 cm increases surface area fourfold (6 → 24 cm²) but volume eightfold (1 → 8 cm³). The SA:V ratio halves (6 → 3 cm⁻¹).

## 3. Why most cells are microscopic
Materials often enter or leave across the cell's surface, while metabolic needs depend substantially on internal volume. As size increases, **the available exchange surface per unit volume decreases**. Smaller cells therefore often exchange nutrients, gases and wastes more efficiently relative to their volume. This is an important constraint, not the only factor governing cell size: transport systems, metabolic rate and cell geometry also matter.

## 4. Ways cells adapt
Cells may remain small, become flattened or elongated, develop folds or projections, or use internal transport. These are explanatory applications of the geometric principle, not separate claims made on the lecture slides.

## Slide Essentials
- For cubes, SA = 6L², V = L³ and SA:V = 6/L.
- Lecture ratios for sides 1, 2 and 4 cm: **6, 3 and 1.5 cm⁻¹**.
- Increasing linear size lowers SA:V, helping explain why most cells are microscopic.

## Concept Connections
**Cell geometry → exchange capacity.** **Plasma membrane → exchange surface.** **Metabolism → volume-related demand.** **Cell specialization → modified geometry.**
