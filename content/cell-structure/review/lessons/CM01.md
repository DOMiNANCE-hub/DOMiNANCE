## Read an original bilayer model from outside to inside
Imagine the sequence: extracellular water → polar heads → nonpolar tail core → polar heads → cytosolic water. Both sets of heads face water, while opposing tails face one another. It is a bilayer because two leaflets orient their amphipathic molecules around a shared hydrophobic interior.
The membrane is not glued into place as a rigid sheet. Lateral movement can occur while the barrier persists, and embedded/associated proteins provide specialised functions. Aqueous cytosol and the membrane's nonpolar interior therefore offer very different environments for an ion or molecule.
**Optional check:** Does “hydrophobic” mean that tails form a separate wall above both layers of heads? **Answer:** No. The tails occupy the bilayer core between the two water-facing head regions.

## Compartment boundaries and the bilayer's physical explanation
Distinguish membrane-bound organelles from organised structures without a surrounding membrane. Nucleus and lysosomes have membranes; ribosomes, nucleoli and centrioles do not have their own enclosing lipid bilayer. The lecturer's statement that each organelle is membrane-enclosed is therefore too broad. Read the earlier general biomembrane description as applying to membrane-bound organelles, not to every named cellular structure.

A phospholipid has polar and nonpolar regions in the same molecule. In water, clustering hydrophobic tails reduces their unfavourable exposure while heads remain hydrated. Two leaflets can enclose a hydrophobic interior without requiring a pre-existing protein shell. This is a free-energy explanation of self-organisation, not a literal claim that tails actively repel water or that every lipid forms the same structure under all conditions.

The bilayer creates a barrier with selective permeability. Small nonpolar molecules can cross more readily than ions; transport proteins provide additional pathways. Lipid and protein movement can coexist with a stable boundary. Cytosol remains a chemically complex aqueous phase rather than pure water, while cytoplasm also includes organelles and other structures outside the nucleus. Approximate water and volume percentages in the slides describe a representative account rather than constants for every cell.

**Optional check:** Does calling the nucleolus an organelle establish that it has a surrounding membrane? **Answer:** No. It is an organised membrane-less region; the membrane statement applies only to membrane-bound organelles.
