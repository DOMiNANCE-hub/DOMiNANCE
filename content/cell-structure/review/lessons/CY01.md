## Actin-associated proteins connect the network to a task
Actin filaments provide thin, polar polymers, but their associated proteins determine how they are arranged and used. Cross-linking and anchoring proteins connect filaments to one another or the membrane; myosin is a force-producing motor that interacts with actin using ATP. A protein that binds actin is therefore not automatically a motor. Polymer assembly can support protrusion, while motor activity can move cargo or slide filaments: these are different ways a network produces movement.

For red cells, short actin filaments form junctions in a flexible spectrin-based membrane skeleton. Spectrin links junctions; ankyrin connects spectrin to the cytoplasmic region of the transmembrane protein band 3. Protein 4.1 helps stabilise junctional connections and membrane attachment. This meshwork supports membrane integrity while permitting deformation. It works with membrane area, cell volume and other factors to maintain the characteristic red-cell geometry; it is not a rigid miniature bone skeleton.

**Optional check:** Is ankyrin the motor that slides actin in muscle? **Answer:** No. Ankyrin links the red-cell skeleton to membrane proteins; myosin is the actin-associated motor in the muscle example.

## Why muscle shortens without shortening each filament
In skeletal muscle, actin-containing thin filaments and myosin-containing thick filaments overlap in repeating sarcomeres. Calcium binds troponin, changing the position of tropomyosin so myosin can bind actin. ATP binding allows myosin to detach; hydrolysis primes the head; product-release-associated changes generate force. Repeated cycles slide thin filaments relative to thick filaments.

The sarcomere shortens as overlap increases. The individual actin and myosin filaments do not have to become shorter. Calcium removal permits binding sites to be covered again, and ATP is also needed for calcium handling and continued cycling. Lack of ATP does not simply cause normal relaxation: cross-bridges can remain attached. This is a skeletal-muscle example; smooth muscle regulates its contractile system differently.

Compare movement systems: myosin acts with actin; kinesin and dynein act with microtubules. Axonemal dynein drives sliding between microtubules in motile cilia, and structural connections convert sliding into bending. These eukaryotic cilia and flagella differ from bacterial rotary flagella. Many sensory primary cilia are nonmotile, so a statement that every cilium beats would be false.

**Optional check:** A sarcomere becomes shorter during contraction. Must its actin filaments have depolymerised? **Answer:** No. Sliding increases overlap of thin and thick filaments; shortening of the whole repeating unit does not require shortening each filament.
