## Use structural evidence and recognise exceptions
Start a comparison with the location of genetic material, then distinguish ribosomes, membranes and walls. A typical bacterium has DNA in a nucleoid without a nuclear envelope and has ribosomes for translation. A typical eukaryotic cell encloses nuclear DNA and has internal membrane compartments. Both have a plasma membrane; absence of a nucleus does not mean absence of DNA or protein synthesis.

| Feature | Interpretation | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear envelope around DNA | Typical eukaryotic organisation | Some specialised eukaryotic cells lose the nucleus |
| Peptidoglycan wall | Characteristic of most bacteria | Some bacteria lack a conventional wall |
| Archaeal wall | Chemically distinct from bacterial peptidoglycan | Wall materials and presence vary |
| Histone-associated DNA | Common in eukaryotes; also occurs in some archaea | Not a universal eukaryote-only test |

A mature red cell lacking a nucleus should therefore not be reclassified as a bacterium. Likewise, a plant cell wall and a bacterial cell wall differ chemically even though both provide support outside the plasma membrane. Use multiple features and the organismal context rather than turning one simplified comparison into an absolute rule.

**Optional check:** An organism lacks a nuclear envelope but has histones. Must it be eukaryotic? **Answer:** No. Some archaea have histones without a membrane-bound nucleus; histones alone do not establish eukaryotic organisation.
