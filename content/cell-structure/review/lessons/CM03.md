## Classify by attachment, orientation and function separately
A transmembrane protein crosses the bilayer and is an integral membrane protein. An integral protein may instead be embedded in only part of the membrane, so integral and transmembrane are not exact synonyms. Peripheral proteins associate with a surface or another protein rather than spanning the hydrophobic core. These terms describe attachment, not whether the molecule is automatically a channel, enzyme or receptor.

Now classify function. A channel provides a selective pathway; a carrier binds solute and changes conformation; a receptor detects a signal and initiates a response. Binding by itself does not make a carrier a receptor, and conformational change by itself does not prove active transport. Some carriers mediate passive movement down a gradient; other transport systems couple movement to an energy source.

Orientation provides another independent distinction. Glycolipid and glycoprotein carbohydrate groups generally face the extracellular side of the plasma membrane, forming part of the glycocalyx. They are not exposed symmetrically to the cytosol and exterior. Cytoskeletal attachments can lie on the cytoplasmic face, linking membrane structure to cell mechanics. A useful diagram must label which side is which rather than placing carbohydrates arbitrarily inside the lipid core.

**Optional check:** Must every integral membrane protein be a receptor that spans the whole bilayer? **Answer:** No. Integral describes membrane association; transmembrane describes crossing the bilayer, and receptor describes a signalling role. These classifications answer different questions.
