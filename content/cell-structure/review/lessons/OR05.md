## Nuclear exchange connects information processing with protein synthesis
The double nuclear envelope separates nuclear processes from cytoplasmic translation. Its outer membrane is continuous with ER; the space between the nuclear membranes connects with the ER lumen. Nuclear pore complexes bridge the envelope and regulate traffic rather than acting as simple holes that admit every molecule smaller than their visible diameter. The source explains that nuclear proteins made in the cytoplasm carry amino-acid targeting signals for selective accumulation.

Trace a typical protein-coding pathway: DNA is transcribed into RNA in the nucleus; processed messenger RNA is exported; cytoplasmic ribosomes translate its sequence; a protein needed in the nucleus must then be imported. Not all transcribed RNA becomes protein: rRNA and tRNA have functional RNA roles. The source's one-gene/one-protein shorthand also omits RNA processing and non-protein-coding genes.

For a chromosome-count example, a usual diploid human somatic cell has 46 chromosomes. After DNA replication, each has two sister chromatids, so DNA content and chromatid number increase while the centromere-based chromosome count remains 46 until sister separation. Gametes are haploid with 23 chromosomes. These counts describe the stated normal context, not every specialised cell or chromosome abnormality.

**Optional check:** Does continuity of the outer nuclear membrane with ER make the nucleoplasm identical to the ER lumen? **Answer:** No. The perinuclear space is continuous with ER lumen; nucleoplasm is separated by the inner nuclear membrane and exchanges through nuclear pores.
