# The twenty common side chains and their chemical behaviour

## Learning goals
Recognise all twenty common residues and their codes. Use functional groups to predict charge, interactions and substitutions, while retaining pH and environment qualifications.

## A usable chemical inventory
The following groups organise common behaviour near neutral pH. Boundaries are teaching conventions rather than sharp universal solubility divisions. G, P and the aromatic residues deserve attention to their individual properties.

| Residue | Codes | Side-chain feature and usual behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glycine | Gly, G | H; small, achiral and flexible |
| Alanine | Ala, A | CH3; nonpolar |
| Valine | Val, V | Branched hydrocarbon; nonpolar |
| Leucine | Leu, L | Branched hydrocarbon; nonpolar |
| Isoleucine | Ile, I | Branched hydrocarbon; additional stereocentre |
| Proline | Pro, P | Ring reconnects to nitrogen; constrained |
| Methionine | Met, M | Thioether; usually grouped as nonpolar |
| Phenylalanine | Phe, F | Phenyl ring; hydrophobic aromatic |
| Tryptophan | Trp, W | Indole ring; aromatic, hydrophobic with an N–H donor |
| Serine | Ser, S | CH2OH; polar uncharged |
| Threonine | Thr, T | Hydroxyl; polar uncharged, additional stereocentre |
| Cysteine | Cys, C | Thiol; polar, can ionise or form disulfides |
| Tyrosine | Tyr, Y | Phenolic OH; aromatic and polar, can ionise |
| Asparagine | Asn, N | Side-chain carboxamide; polar uncharged |
| Glutamine | Gln, Q | Longer carboxamide; polar uncharged |
| Aspartate | Asp, D | Side-chain carboxylate; usually negative |
| Glutamate | Glu, E | Longer carboxylate; usually negative |
| Lysine | Lys, K | Terminal side-chain amino group; usually positive |
| Arginine | Arg, R | Guanidinium; usually positive |
| Histidine | His, H | Imidazole; protonation sensitive near biological pH |

Aspartic acid/aspartate and glutamic acid/glutamate name different protonation forms of the same respective amino-acid connectivity. “Acidic residue” describes the side-chain acid chemistry; at pH 7 the common side-chain state is negatively charged. Asparagine and glutamine have amides instead, so they do not carry the corresponding carboxylate charge.

## Predict interactions from groups
Hydrocarbon-rich groups tend to avoid exposure to water and often pack inside soluble proteins. Polar OH or amide groups can hydrogen-bond with water or other protein groups. Lys/Arg can attract carboxylates electrostatically. An interaction depends on distance, orientation, solvent and protonation; two oppositely charged residues on distant surfaces do not automatically form a salt bridge.

Histidine is conventionally grouped as basic, but its side chain is often substantially uncharged at pH 7. With an illustrative imidazolium pKa of 6, only about 9% is protonated at pH 7 in a simple model. A protein environment can shift that pKa. The ability to accept and donate a proton helps explain histidine's frequent participation in catalytic proton transfers.

Cysteine has a thiol that can be deprotonated to a nucleophilic thiolate or oxidised with another cysteine to form cystine's disulfide. Methionine contains a thioether and cannot form that same ordinary cysteine disulfide. Tyrosine's phenol and tryptophan's indole distinguish them from phenylalanine even though all three are aromatic.

## Worked substitution reasoning
Replacing a solvent-exposed glutamate with glutamine preserves a similar-sized polar side chain but removes the usual negative charge. Replacing it with valine removes both the carboxylate and much of the hydrogen-bonding capacity, creating a more hydrophobic patch. Neither substitution guarantees loss of function: location, packing, interacting partners and biological conditions determine the consequence. This is stronger reasoning than declaring every mutation “bad” or every similar-size substitution harmless.

For a hypothetical Lys–Gly–Asp segment at near-neutral pH, side chains contribute approximately +1+0−1=0. That is not the complete charge unless terminal groups, other residues and actual ionisation have also been considered.

## Short concept checks
- Why is glutamine not grouped with negatively charged glutamate at neutral pH? **Answer:** Its side chain is a carboxamide rather than a carboxylate; it remains polar but is normally uncharged there.
- Which sulfur residue directly supplies a thiol for an ordinary disulfide: cysteine or methionine? **Answer:** Cysteine. Methionine's sulfur is in a thioether.

## Key takeaways
Learn names and codes alongside functional groups. Side-chain chemistry predicts possibilities; pH and the folded environment determine actual interactions.
