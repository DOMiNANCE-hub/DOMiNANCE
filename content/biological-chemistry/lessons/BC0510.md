# Storage-glucan cleavage and the iodine comparison

## Learning goals
Distinguish hydrolysis from phosphorolysis and explain endo/exo action. Interpret the iodine–starch result as a conformation-dependent signal rather than a universal carbohydrate test.

## Different reagents yield different products
Hydrolysis cleaves a glycosidic linkage using water. Phosphorolysis uses inorganic phosphate and can release glucose 1-phosphate from suitable nonreducing chain ends. The lecturer's starch-phosphorylase diagram explicitly labels α-D-glucose 1-phosphate, so the accompanying phrase split into monosaccharides must not be interpreted as necessarily releasing free glucose. Sugar phosphate and free sugar enter different downstream steps.

α-Amylase acts internally on suitable α(1→4) linkages, producing shorter chains and products including maltose and maltotriose; it does not directly convert every starch residue into free glucose. β-Amylase is an exo-acting enzyme in the plant/microbial examples that releases maltose from suitable nonreducing ends. Neither name means universal cleavage of α(1→6) branch bonds. Debranching and additional enzymes are needed for more complete digestion.

Animal salivary and pancreatic α-amylases act in different digestive locations. Acid conditions reduce salivary amylase activity as digestion proceeds into the stomach, while pancreatic enzyme functions in the small intestine. Actual rates depend on conditions and accessibility; cooked starch can become more accessible through granule swelling/gelatinisation, but processing and cooling can also change digestibility. No fixed digestion rate is inferred from sugar composition alone.

## Glycogen mobilisation in context
Glycogen phosphorylase releases glucose 1-phosphate from α(1→4) chains and stops near branch regions; other activities handle branches. Muscle glycogen supports local metabolic demand, while liver glycogen contributes to blood-glucose support through additional pathways. The presence of glycogen does not mean every tissue can release free glucose into blood in the same way.

## Why iodine produces a starch-associated colour
Amylose can adopt a helix that accommodates iodine/polyiodide species, producing a characteristic blue to blue-black complex under suitable conditions. The signal depends on chain organisation as well as chemical composition. It is not the same reaction as reducing Cu²⁺ in Benedict's test, and free glucose does not provide an amylose helix. Different glucans and conditions can give different colours or intensity.

For a reasoning exercise, compare untreated starch with a sample extensively cleaved into short sugars under a controlled procedure. Loss of long helical regions can weaken the characteristic iodine signal even while total carbohydrate remains. Heating can also alter complex organisation, so a colour change alone does not prove bond cleavage; a cooling comparison and reaction controls matter. The lecture does not supply a calibrated quantitative iodine assay or a complete laboratory protocol.

## Short concept checks
- Which reactant distinguishes phosphorolysis from ordinary hydrolysis? **Answer:** Inorganic phosphate rather than water is used in the cleavage described, yielding a sugar phosphate such as glucose 1-phosphate.
- Does a negative characteristic iodine–amylose colour establish absence of all carbohydrates? **Answer:** No. Small sugars and altered chain organisations may lack that signal while carbohydrate remains present.

## Key takeaways
Specify enzyme, bond, attacking reagent and product. A visual test detects a particular chemical/structural response rather than certifying every carbohydrate property.
