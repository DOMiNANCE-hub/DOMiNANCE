# Zymogens, activation cascades and isoenzymes

## Learning goals
Explain why enzymes can be made as inactive precursors, distinguish irreversible proteolytic activation from reversible regulation and interpret isoenzyme composition without making diagnostic claims.

## Proteolysis can activate rather than merely destroy
A zymogen or proenzyme is an inactive precursor that can become catalytically active after specific processing, often cleavage of selected peptide bonds. Cleavage can remove an inhibitory segment or permit a new active-site arrangement. It does not mean every peptide bond is hydrolysed or that all structure is lost. Mature chains or fragments can remain associated in the functioning protein.

Pepsinogen→pepsin and trypsinogen→trypsin are useful digestive examples. Production, storage, compartmentation and regulated activation help limit unwanted proteolysis in the tissues producing the precursors. Pancreatic digestive zymogens are not all normally fully active within pancreatic cells merely because their amino-acid sequences have been synthesised.

Proteolytic activation is effectively irreversible for that precursor molecule in the ordinary regulatory cycle: removing the activating protease does not reattach the excised peptide. Inactivation, inhibition, degradation and new synthesis can control the active enzyme later. This differs from reversible phosphorylation or reversible noncovalent ligand binding.

## Activation cascades amplify a signal
One active protease can activate several downstream precursor molecules, which can each act on further targets. This produces amplification and creates several points for spatial and inhibitory control. If a hypothetical upstream active enzyme activates ten downstream molecules, and each downstream molecule activates twenty further molecules, the ideal no-loss count is 10×20=200, not thirty. Real cascades have timing, depletion, competing inhibitors and localisation constraints, so that multiplication is a constructed upper-scope example rather than measured physiology.

Blood-clotting systems illustrate coordinated protease activation, but the classroom intrinsic/extrinsic division is not a complete description of coagulation in vivo. Exact factor inventories and diagnostic interpretation need their own evidence. Not every inactive precursor is a zymogen: proinsulin processing produces a hormone rather than activating an enzyme catalyst. This preserves the useful processing analogy without misclassifying insulin as an enzyme.

## Isoenzymes catalyse the same reaction with different molecular forms
Isoenzymes are distinguishable molecular forms with the same overall catalytic activity but differences in sequence, assembly, regulation or kinetic behaviour. They need not all arise solely from different subunit combinations, nor must every isoenzyme be a multimer.

The lecturer's common LDH model uses A and B subunits in a tetramer. The compositions A4, A3B, A2B2, AB3 and B4 form five possibilities when only counts of the two subunit types are distinguished. This is not sixteen distinct isoenzymes from treating all four positions as permanently distinguishable. If geometry or other gene products matter, more information is required; the model does not claim an exhaustive inventory of every LDH-related protein.

## Worked composition and interpretation
A3B contains four subunits, three of type A and one of type B. It does not mean three active sites plus one inactive inhibitor, or an α helix paired with a β sheet. Molecular forms can have different substrate dependence, product sensitivity or tissue expression while sharing the overall reaction. Relative expression can help match metabolic function to cellular context.

A tissue-associated isoenzyme pattern does not by itself prove a diagnosis or the exact origin of every molecule in a mixed sample. Sample handling, assay method, other tissues and biological context matter. This lesson uses the composition model to explain biochemical diversity; no patient assay or diagnostic validation was performed.

## Short concept checks
- Does stopping the activating protease automatically turn mature trypsin back into trypsinogen? **Answer:** No. Proteolytic processing is not reversed by simply removing the activating enzyme.
- How many count-distinguished A/B tetramer compositions occur in the supplied simple LDH model? **Answer:** Five: A4, A3B, A2B2, AB3 and B4.

## Key takeaways
Selective cleavage can create function; cascades amplify and require control. Isoenzymes share catalytic activity while differing molecularly, and a teaching model is not a complete clinical diagnostic rule.
