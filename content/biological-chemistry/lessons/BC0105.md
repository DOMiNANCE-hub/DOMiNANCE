# Biological selection from the periodic table

## Learning goals
Explain why environmental abundance is insufficient to predict cellular composition. Evaluate “chemical democracy” as a descriptive phrase and distinguish explanatory evidence from resemblance.

## Availability, chemistry and regulated selection
Cells depend on available matter, but they are not passive samples of their surroundings. Membranes, transport systems, binding sites and metabolic pathways select among chemical species. Some substances are taken up, others excluded, stored or excreted. The resulting composition reflects environmental availability together with chemical suitability and regulated biological processes.

Many prominent biological elements are small nonmetals able to form covalent bonds. This helps explain the chemistry of organic molecules, but does not explain every essential element. Sodium and potassium contribute to ionic gradients; transition metals can support specialised protein functions. Hydrogen is a prominent exception to describing the major elements as all p-block elements: it occupies the first position in the periodic table and is conventionally placed in the s block.

“Chemical democracy” describes broad representation of elements across the periodic table in biological systems. Biological importance does not simply follow environmental abundance. It is not a mechanism, a rule that all elements contribute equally or a proof that organisms deliberately choose elements. A useful explanation specifies the actual chemical property or cellular process. For example, a membrane transporter can discriminate between ions because binding and movement depend on their charge, size, hydration and protein interactions.

Comparisons between seawater, Earth's crust and human material may reveal striking differences. Those samples have different histories and denominators, so a resemblance is a starting observation rather than a complete evolutionary argument. Demonstrating ancestry requires independent evidence; demonstrating essentiality requires functional evidence.

## Worked interpretation
An environment contains abundant element A and only a small amount of element B. A cell retains B strongly because a protein uses it at a specialised site, while A has no equivalent role and is largely excluded. The observation is compatible with selective uptake and retention. It does not imply that B is abundant in the environment, that all species need B or that increasing B intake without limit improves function.

If a figure reports a body percentage and a seawater concentration, first check whether both use comparable units. A mass percentage cannot be ranked directly against an amount per litre without conversion and a defined comparison. Next distinguish the observation from the proposed explanation. The graph may establish a difference; it cannot by itself identify the transporter, evolutionary cause or dietary requirement responsible.

## Exceptions and source cautions
The lecturer groups several radioactive elements with elements of atomic number greater than 92. That grouping requires correction: technetium has Z = 43, astatine Z = 85 and francium Z = 87. Radioactivity does not mean “transuranic”. Transuranic means atomic number greater than uranium's 92. Neither classification alone establishes a substance's biological role or the size of an exposure risk.

## Short concept checks
- Is hydrogen a p-block element because it appears in major biomolecules? **Answer:** No. Biological role does not determine periodic-table block; hydrogen is conventionally in the s block.
- Does similar elemental composition prove a particular evolutionary relationship? **Answer:** No. It is an observation requiring independent historical and biological evidence for that conclusion.

## Key takeaways
Availability constrains biology, while chemistry and regulation influence selection. Interpret composition figures with their units and scope. Descriptive labels and resemblance cannot replace mechanisms or independent evidence.
