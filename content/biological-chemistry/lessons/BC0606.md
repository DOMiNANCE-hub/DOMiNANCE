# UV measurements, denaturation and melting temperature

## Learning goals
Interpret A260 measurements, purity ratios, hyperchromicity and melting curves. Perform explicitly calibrated examples while distinguishing calculated values from an executed experiment.

## Absorbance is useful but not uniquely identifying
Nucleobases absorb ultraviolet light strongly near 260 nm. Measured absorbance depends on concentration, path length, molecular state and other absorbing or scattering material. A260 is therefore useful only with the appropriate blank, dilution and calibration. Strong UV absorption does not mean every absorbed photon creates a mutation; wavelength, exposure and photochemistry matter.

The lecturer gives approximate A260/A280 reference values around 1.8 for DNA and 2.0 for RNA. These are screening guides, not proof of complete purity, intact sequence or downstream assay suitability. pH, salts, contaminants and low-concentration measurement error can affect ratios. DNA with an apparently suitable ratio can still be fragmented; integrity and purity require different evidence.

For an illustrative sample A260=.90 and A280=.50, the ratio is 1.8. This is compatible with the stated guide but does not certify integrity. If a problem explicitly supplies a calibration of 50 µg/mL per A260 unit for the specified double-stranded DNA, a 1:20 diluted sample (one part sample brought to twenty parts total) with A260=.20 corresponds to .20×50×20=200 µg/mL in the original. Here absorbances are blank-corrected and normalised to the calibration path length, such as 1 cm. The factor must be supplied or appropriately validated; it is not interchangeable across all DNA, RNA and free nucleotide samples.

## Denaturation and the hyperchromic effect
Heat or altered solution conditions can disrupt duplex organisation and separate strands. Ordinary thermal denaturation does not require cleavage of the covalent backbone. Disrupting base stacking changes electronic interactions and commonly raises absorbance near 260 nm: the hyperchromic effect. It is not a guarantee of exactly 40% increase at every wavelength, despite the slide's broad wording.

If an illustrative A260 rises from .50 to .70, the relative increase is (.70−.50)/.50×100=40%. A change from .50 to .60 is 20%, even though the latter value might also be the midpoint signal of that particular .50-to-.70 transition. Percentage change and fraction of the total melting signal are different calculations.

## Worked melting-curve interpretation
For low and high plateaus of .50 and .70, half the signal change is at A260=.60. If that midpoint occurs at 70°C in the stated illustrative curve, its operational Tm is 70°C. Tm is not the first temperature at which any base pair opens and not the midpoint between zero absorbance and the high plateau. Native/denatured baselines may vary with temperature in actual measurements and require suitable analysis.

Melting can be cooperative: destabilisation in one region facilitates nearby opening. Tm also depends on length, sequence, mismatches, solvent, ions and, for relevant duplex equilibria, concentration. Cooling can permit annealing when complementary strands and suitable conditions are present, but incorrect conditions do not guarantee rapid complete restoration. No UV spectrum or melting experiment was performed for these theoretical examples.

## Short concept checks
- Does an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8 alone prove intact DNA? **Answer:** No. It is a screening ratio; integrity needs additional evidence such as a suitable size-distribution measurement.
- Where is the half-change signal for plateaus .50 and .70? **Answer:** .60, after accounting for the lower baseline rather than taking half of .70.

## Key takeaways
Specify calibration, baselines, wavelength and conditions. A ratio, a percent increase and an operational melting midpoint provide different information.
