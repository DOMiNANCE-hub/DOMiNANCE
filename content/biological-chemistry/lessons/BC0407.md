# Constitutional isomers, tautomers and representation

## Learning goals
Distinguish different connectivity from a different drawing of the same molecule. Classify chain, positional and functional isomerism, and explain why tautomerism differs from resonance.

## Same formula does not determine structure
A molecular formula counts atoms but does not specify how they connect. Constitutional, or structural, isomers have the same molecular formula and different connectivity. Their physical and chemical properties can differ because bonding patterns and functional groups differ. Before classifying a pair, confirm both formula and connectivity rather than relying on a change in drawing orientation.

Chain isomerism changes the carbon framework. Butane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃, and 2-methylpropane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃, are both C₄H₁₀ but have straight and branched connectivities. Positional isomerism keeps a broad framework and functional-group type while changing its position, as in propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol. Functional isomerism changes the functional-group arrangement, as in ethanol and dimethyl ether, both C₂H₆O.

These categories help organise examples but are not independent physical forces. The actual bond network explains the difference. A rotated butane drawing remains butane; it does not become a branched constitutional isomer simply because part of the chain looks bent on paper.

## Worked formula and connectivity check
Compare CH₃CH₂OH with CH₃OCH₃. Each has two carbons, six hydrogens and one oxygen. In the first, oxygen bonds to carbon and hydrogen, giving an alcohol. In the second, oxygen joins two carbon groups, giving an ether. The alcohol has an O–H donor; the ether does not. This supplies a molecular reason for differing interaction patterns without changing the molecular formula.

Now compare CH₃CH₂CH₂OH with CH₃CH(OH)CH₃. Count three carbons, eight hydrogens and one oxygen in each. The OH group attaches at an end versus the middle of the chain. Redrawing the first from right to left would not move its OH group to the middle, so these are genuinely different connectivities.

## Tautomers are distinct interconverting species
Keto–enol tautomerism involves movement of a proton and relocation of a double bond, producing different connectivities that can interconvert through a chemical process. A simple example is propanone, CH₃–C(=O)–CH₃, and its enol, CH₂=C(OH)–CH₃. Both are C₃H₆O, but they are not equally abundant merely because both can be drawn. Their equilibrium depends on structure and conditions, and catalysis can affect the rate of interconversion.

Resonance contributors differ in electron placement while retaining the same nuclear positions and connectivity. They are not separate molecules exchanging a proton. Tautomerism therefore must not be described as just another way of drawing the same electronic state. This distinction becomes important for nucleic-acid bases and the interpretation of chemical reactions, without implying that every possible tautomer has the same biological frequency.

## Short concept checks
- Are ethanol and dimethyl ether constitutional isomers? **Answer:** Yes. Both are C₂H₆O, but their oxygen connectivity and functional-group type differ.
- Does keto–enol interconversion leave all nuclei in the same connectivity, as resonance does? **Answer:** No. Proton attachment and bond placement change, so the tautomers are distinct interconverting species.

## Key takeaways
Check formula, then connectivity, then three-dimensional arrangement. A change in drawing orientation, a conformer, a resonance contributor and a tautomer are different kinds of representation or chemical relationship.
