# Waves, orbitals and probability

## Learning goals
Distinguish an orbital from an orbit. Interpret a probability-density representation and explain the uncertainty principle without treating it as merely an instrument defect.

## The quantum description
Electrons show both particle-like and wave-like behaviour. For a particle with momentum p, the de Broglie relationship is wavelength λ = h/p, where h is Planck's constant. Wavelength is therefore related to momentum; it is not another name for position. A fixed planetary path cannot capture the full observed behaviour of electrons in atoms.

Solutions of the atomic wave equation are wavefunctions, usually written ψ. The probability density is |ψ|². To obtain a probability for a region, this density must be integrated over that region; a density at one point is not itself the probability of finding the electron at that exact mathematical point. An atomic orbital describes a one-electron quantum state and its spatial wavefunction. It is not a hard container or a path that the electron must follow.

Pictures often enclose a chosen proportion of the probability distribution, such as 90% or 95%. That drawn boundary is a convention, not a physical wall. A small probability remains outside it. An s-orbital probability distribution is spherically symmetric; p orbitals are commonly represented with two lobes separated by a nodal plane. Different colours or signs on orbital lobes indicate wavefunction phase, not opposite electrical charges. Every electron has the same negative charge.

The uncertainty relation for one direction is Δx Δp_x ≥ ħ/2. It limits how narrowly a state can specify position and the corresponding momentum together. Improved instruments cannot turn a quantum orbital into a simultaneously exact position-and-momentum orbit. This does not mean every possible property of an electron is completely unknowable or that measurements have no predictive value.

## Worked photon calculation
An illustrative spectral line has wavelength 500 nm. Convert first: 500 nm = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m. Using E = hc/λ with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s gives E = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon. Doubling the wavelength to 1,000 nm halves the photon energy. The calculation connects spectral energy differences to measured light; it is a supplementary worked example, not a claim that this numerical line is an official lecturer examination item.

Check units: J s multiplied by m/s and divided by m leaves joules. Inserting 500 without converting nanometres would give an answer wrong by a factor of a billion. Also distinguish photon energy from an electron's kinetic energy; E = hc/λ here concerns light.

## Comparing representations
A Bohr ring depicts a permitted orbit within that historical model. A modern orbital drawing depicts spatial probability information. A probability boundary does not establish a precise electron trajectory, and a larger drawn orbital does not necessarily mean more electrons. Orbital occupancy requires separate rules, developed next.

The lecturer's “95% volume” definition is retained as an explanation of one drawing convention, not as the exact mathematical definition of every orbital. “Position (wavelength)” and literal circulating electron paths are not adopted.

## Short concept checks
- Does a lobe of opposite wavefunction phase contain positively charged electrons? **Answer:** No. Phase is a wavefunction property; electron charge remains negative.
- What happens to photon energy when wavelength doubles? **Answer:** It halves, because E = hc/λ.

## Key takeaways
Orbitals describe quantum states and probability distributions, not miniature planetary tracks. Keep wavelength, position and momentum distinct; use |ψ|² and an explicit spatial region when interpreting probability.
