# Enzymes, activation barriers and equilibrium

## Learning goals
Explain biological catalysis, distinguish kinetic barriers from thermodynamic driving force and calculate a relative catalytic rate from supplied comparable constants.

## A favourable reaction can still be slow
An enzyme increases the rate of a reaction by providing a suitable catalytic pathway. Most enzymes are proteins; some catalytic molecules are RNA, called ribozymes. A substrate is a reactant transformed by the enzyme. The enzyme can participate in intermediate chemical steps but is regenerated in a complete catalytic cycle, rather than being consumed once for each product molecule.

A negative reaction free-energy change means the forward direction is thermodynamically favourable at the specified conditions; it does not specify how quickly the reaction occurs. Reactants must pass through higher-free-energy configurations along a reaction coordinate. The activation free energy ΔG‡ describes a barrier relative to the starting state. ΔG describes the difference between products and reactants. Confusing these quantities would imply that a slowly reacting fuel has little available thermodynamic driving force.

The lecturer's glucose example illustrates this distinction: oxidation can release substantial free energy while an uncatalysed mixture reacts slowly under mild conditions. “Stored forever without deterioration” is too absolute: humidity, microbes and chemical conditions affect storage. The quoted standard energy is not a universal cellular ΔG independent of concentrations, temperature or standard-state convention.

## Catalysts change pathways, not the equilibrium endpoint
An enzyme stabilises appropriate transition-state arrangements and can use several elementary steps instead of one high-barrier route. A transition state is a fleeting configuration at a barrier, not an ordinary isolatable stable enzyme–substrate intermediate. The catalysed and uncatalysed routes have the same initial and final chemical states, so the enzyme alone does not change their free-energy difference or equilibrium constant.

Catalysis accelerates approach to equilibrium in both directions. At equilibrium, forward and reverse molecular reactions can continue at equal rates while net change is zero. Increasing enzyme amount cannot drive an isolated reaction beyond its equilibrium composition. Coupling it to another reaction or maintaining concentrations by transport can change the overall thermodynamic situation; that is a different intervention from simply lowering a barrier.

## Worked catalytic-rate comparison
For an explicitly supplied pair of comparable first-order rate constants, let kcat-like rate=3×10^4 s−1 and uncatalysed rate=3×10−10 s−1. Their ratio is (3/3)×10^(4−(−10))=10^14. The ratio is dimensionless because the units cancel. It says how much faster the defined comparison is under those conditions, not how much product accumulates at equilibrium or how many times more free energy is released.

Comparisons require like quantities, substrate definitions and assay conditions. One cannot divide an amount/time assay reading by a first-order constant and call the result a dimensionless enhancement. Nor is a very large enhancement a universal ranking proving that every enzyme outperforms every synthetic catalyst under every condition.

## Biological significance
Fast, controlled transformations allow cells to sustain linked pathways under relatively mild conditions. Catalytic speed must be paired with regulation: an enzyme at a branch can influence distribution between products, and a cell can alter enzyme abundance or activity. A pathway's flux is not determined by one isolated maximum rate alone; substrate supply, competing reactions, compartmentation and thermodynamics matter.

## Short concept checks
- Does lowering ΔG‡ necessarily make the reaction ΔG more negative? **Answer:** No. A catalyst changes the pathway/barriers without changing the free-energy difference between the same initial and final states.
- At equilibrium, does zero net rate require all molecular reactions to stop? **Answer:** No. Forward and reverse rates can be equal and nonzero.

## Key takeaways
Rate, barrier height and reaction driving force are distinct. Enzymes accelerate equilibration; coupling and concentration control must be analysed separately.
