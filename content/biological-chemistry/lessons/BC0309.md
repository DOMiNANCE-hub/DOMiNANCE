# Electron addition, electronegativity and sign conventions

## Learning goals
Distinguish electron affinity from electronegativity. Interpret an explicit electron-addition energy convention and explain why broad trends have exceptions.

## Two related but different questions
Electron-addition energy concerns a gaseous species gaining an electron, for example Cl(g) + e− → Cl−(g). Here we use the energy-change convention: a negative ΔE means the process releases energy, while positive ΔE means energy input is required. Some tables define electron affinity as positive energy released for a favourable addition. Always read the convention before comparing numerical signs. “Higher affinity” can otherwise ambiguously mean a more negative energy change or a larger released-energy magnitude.

Attraction for an incoming electron depends on nuclear charge, shielding, electron population and subshell structure. Many elements toward the right of a period have favourable first additions, but the pattern is less uniform than a single trend arrow suggests. Closed or half-filled subshell arrangements and repulsion in compact orbitals produce exceptions.

Chlorine's first electron addition is more exothermic than fluorine's in the usual comparison, even though fluorine is more electronegative. Fluorine's compact 2p shell creates substantial electron–electron repulsion for the incoming electron. This does not imply that chlorine attracts every bonded electron pair more strongly than fluorine; electron addition to an isolated gaseous atom is a different process from competition for electrons within a bond.

Electronegativity describes an atom's tendency to attract shared electron density in a chemical bond. The Pauling scale is comparative and dimensionless, with fluorine close to 4.0. It is not a directly measured electron-addition energy in kJ/mol. Bonding environment and scale matter, and differences help discuss bond polarity without creating a universal sharp boundary between ionic and covalent bonding.

## Worked first and second addition
The lecturer gives rounded illustrative values for oxygen additions: O(g) + e− → O−(g), ΔE = −141 kJ/mol; then O−(g) + e− → O²−(g), ΔE = +798 kJ/mol. Using this stated convention, the combined two-electron addition change is −141 + 798 = +657 kJ/mol. The second step is opposed by adding a negative electron to an already negative ion.

The calculation does not prove oxide-containing solids cannot form. A complete energetic account includes interactions and other steps, such as lattice formation, which can compensate for an unfavourable isolated-ion step. It also does not describe producing a bare O²− ion inside a living cell. Keep gaseous atomic energetics separate from aqueous and solid-state chemistry.

| Property | Process or context | Typical reporting |
|---|---|---|
| Ionisation energy | Remove an electron from a specified gaseous species | Positive removal energy, often kJ/mol |
| Electron-addition energy | Add an electron to a specified gaseous species | Signed energy change; convention must be stated |
| Electronegativity | Attract shared electrons in a bond | Comparative dimensionless scale |

## Common traps and scope
“More negative ΔE” means more exothermic in the convention used here, not more energy required. A favourable first addition does not guarantee a favourable second addition. Electronegativity and electron affinity cannot be substituted for one another merely because both concern electrons. Exact numbers in different reference tables may vary with rounding and definition; the worked oxygen sum uses the lecturer's stated values and is not a new precision claim.

## Short concept checks
- In the stated ΔE convention, does +798 kJ/mol mean release or input? **Answer:** Energy input; the second electron addition is endothermic.
- Why can fluorine be more electronegative while chlorine has a more exothermic first electron addition? **Answer:** Bonded-electron attraction and isolated-atom electron addition are different properties; compact-shell repulsion affects the latter.

## Key takeaways
Specify process, species and sign convention before comparing values. Electron addition and bond polarity are related through electronic structure but are not the same measurement or concept.
