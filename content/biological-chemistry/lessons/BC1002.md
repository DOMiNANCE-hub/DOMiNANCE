# Fatty-acid structure, numbering and nomenclature

## Learning goals
Read carbon:number-of-double-bonds notation, locate double bonds from either end and calculate simple formula changes with explicit structural assumptions.

## A carboxyl head and a hydrocarbon chain
An ordinary fatty acid contains a terminal carboxyl group and a hydrocarbon chain. The carboxyl carbon is included in the total carbon count. Free fatty acids are often substantially ionised at physiological pH, but local environment, aggregation and binding can affect apparent acid–base behaviour. Esterified fatty-acid-derived groups are acyl residues, not independent free carboxylates at every lipid position.

Saturated describes the absence of carbon–carbon double bonds in the ordinary chain, not the absence of the carboxyl C=O. Monounsaturated means one C=C; polyunsaturated means two or more. Natural fatty acids often have even carbon counts, reflecting common biosynthetic building patterns, but odd, branched, cyclic and other modified chains exist.

## Read the carbon and double-bond counts first
Stearic acid is 18:0, an eighteen-carbon saturated fatty acid, also called octadecanoic acid. Palmitic acid is 16:0. Oleic acid is commonly described as cis-18:1Δ9: eighteen carbons, one double bond between carboxyl-numbered carbons 9 and 10. The bare shorthand 18:1 does not by itself specify position or cis/trans geometry.

Delta numbering starts at the carboxyl carbon as C1. Omega or n numbering refers to the methyl end; an n-3 fatty acid has its first double bond three carbons from that end. These are different coordinate conventions, not different molecules when used consistently. A complete description may need chain length, all double-bond positions and geometry.

## Worked conversions between conventions
For an eighteen-carbon chain with Δ9,12 double bonds, the methyl-nearest double bond is the one with the highest delta number, 12. The omega family is n−12=18−12=6: 18:2n-6. For Δ9,12,15, it is 18−15=3, giving 18:3n-3. For Δ6,9,12, it is again n-6 despite having three double bonds.

Consequently, α-linolenic acid, 18:3Δ9,12,15, is n-3, whereas γ-linolenic acid, 18:3Δ6,9,12, is n-6. Equal chain length and double-bond count do not make them identical. The omega label locates the first double bond from the methyl end; it does not mean the molecule has three or six double bonds.

## Formula and degree-of-unsaturation bookkeeping
For an ordinary acyclic unbranched monocarboxylic acid with no additional functional groups, the saturated formula is CnH2nO2. Each additional C=C removes two hydrogens relative to that saturated comparator. Thus an eighteen-carbon saturated example is C18H36O2, while an eighteen-carbon chain with two additional C=C bonds is C18H32O2.

This is a structural bookkeeping rule under stated assumptions, not a universal formula for every oxidised, cyclic or multifunctional lipid. A ring also changes hydrogen count, and an esterified acyl group is not the same free-acid formula. Always name the species before balancing a lipid reaction.

## Recognising important examples without a false limit
Linoleic acid is 18:2n-6; α-linolenic acid is 18:3n-3. Arachidonic acid is commonly 20:4n-6. EPA is 20:5n-3 and DHA 22:6n-3, showing why the lecturer's prose limiting fatty-acid double bonds to four is not universal; its own next figure includes EPA and DHA. These examples provide structural anchors, not a dietary dosing table.

## Short concept checks
- What does 18:3n-3 specify? **Answer:** Eighteen carbons, three C=C bonds and the methyl-nearest double bond at the n-3 position; further detail can specify all positions and geometry.
- Why do 18:3Δ9,12,15 and 18:3Δ6,9,12 belong to different omega families? **Answer:** Their methyl-nearest double bonds differ: 18−15=3 versus 18−12=6.

## Key takeaways
Count from the stated end and include the carboxyl carbon. Chain length, unsaturation count, position and geometry are separate information.
