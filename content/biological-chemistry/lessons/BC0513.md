# Acid-dehydration carbohydrate tests and interpretation

## Learning goals
Compare Molisch, Bial and Seliwanoff principles and interpret controlled observations without treating a screening colour as unique identification. Distinguish chemical theory from an executed laboratory check.

## Shared starting chemistry, different colour reagents
Strong acid can dehydrate suitable carbohydrates to furfural-related products. Pentoses yield furfural; hexoses can yield hydroxymethylfurfural. Subsequent condensation with an appropriate aromatic reagent produces a coloured product. Conditions and timing affect what is observed; a reagent-specific response is not a direct photograph of the original sugar molecule.

Molisch's test is a broad carbohydrate screen using α-naphthol and strong acid, with a violet ring or coloured interface under the specified laboratory conditions. The source prose calls the naphthol sulfonated, whereas its adjacent reaction diagram identifies α-naphthol. The standard teaching reagent is α-naphthol; that adjective is not adopted as a defining required component. Glycoconjugates or other carbohydrate-containing samples can respond, so a positive test does not identify glucose specifically.

## Bial and Seliwanoff distinctions
Bial's reaction uses orcinol in acid with ferric ions; pentose-derived furfural gives a characteristic green/blue-green response. The source also describes the coloured product as soluble in butanol; that partition observation is not a unique sugar identity test. Hexoses can produce other colours under some conditions, so the assay relies on the prescribed comparison rather than a rule that every non-green sample contains no sugar. Ribose is a suitable pentose example; glucose is a hexose comparison.

Seliwanoff's reaction uses resorcinol in acid. Ketoses generally develop a cherry-red response faster than aldoses under controlled heating. The distinction is kinetic: prolonged heating, rearrangement or hydrolysis can reduce its specificity. Sucrose can yield fructose during acid hydrolysis and then give a response, so a positive result need not mean that the original intact sample was a free ketose monosaccharide.

## Worked sequence and controls
Suppose an unknown gives a broad Molisch response, has a rapid Seliwanoff response compared with an aldose reference, and gives an alkaline reducing reaction. A ketose such as fructose is compatible with the pattern. The pattern does not uniquely identify fructose: mixtures, hydrolysable materials, concentration and handling need consideration. A reagent blank detects background; a known positive verifies reagent performance; a suitable comparison tests the intended discrimination.

Use identical sample amounts, defined heating and observation conditions from an approved practical protocol. This lesson deliberately supplies no invented universal time cutoff or reagent recipe. Concentrated acid and heated reagents require supervised laboratory arrangements; these explanations describe expected chemistry, not reported practical observations. A result must be obtained and recorded during the actual supervised practical.

## Short concept checks
- Does a violet Molisch interface uniquely identify glucose? **Answer:** No. It is a broad carbohydrate-associated response; additional evidence is needed for identity.
- Why is heating time important in Seliwanoff comparisons? **Answer:** The useful distinction is faster ketose colour development under controlled conditions; prolonged reactions and hydrolysis can produce misleading classifications.

## Key takeaways
Explain substrate chemistry, colour-forming reagent, conditions and limits together. A control pattern supports an interpretation without replacing independent identification.
