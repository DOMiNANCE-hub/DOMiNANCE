# Amino-acid reactions, detection and spectroscopy

## Learning goals
Relate analytical reactions to functional groups, recognise ninhydrin exceptions, distinguish electronic from vibrational absorption and interpret measurements without claiming an experiment was performed.

## Different groups give different reactions
Proton transfer between an amino group and a carboxylic acid can produce a salt. Forming a peptide requires a new covalent amide linkage and suitable activation; it is not simply that acid–base exchange. A carboxyl group can be esterified with an alcohol under suitable conditions, while free primary amino groups can undergo acylation or form imines, often called Schiff bases, with appropriate carbonyl compounds. Reaction conditions and protonation determine reactivity: the diagrams are connectivity summaries, not instructions that any mixed aqueous sample instantly gives all products.

A cysteine thiolate can react with suitable electrophiles, including maleimides or iodoacetate, to modify a thiol. Oxidation can join two cysteine thiols as a disulfide; reduction reverses that linkage. These reactions differ from changing the peptide backbone. Accessibility matters: a buried group, an already oxidised cysteine or a competing reagent changes interpretation. Specificity is conditional, so a reagent name alone does not prove an exclusive single-site reaction in a complex sample.

## Ninhydrin detects chemistry, not identity by itself
For ordinary free primary α-amino acids, ninhydrin chemistry includes oxidative deamination and decarboxylation. It produces an aldehyde with one fewer carbon than the original amino acid, CO2 and ammonia; further chemistry yields the blue-purple product commonly called Ruhemann's purple, measured near 570 nm under standard conditions. For glycine, the corresponding aldehyde is formaldehyde. Free proline and hydroxyproline, with secondary amino groups, give a different yellow product rather than the same ordinary purple response.

A positive colour therefore does not uniquely identify a particular amino acid or establish equal amounts in different samples. Appropriate blanks, standards, concentration ranges and reaction conditions are required. Other amino-containing compounds can react. Ninhydrin can reveal chromatographically separated amino acids; the separation and the detection supply different information.

## UV and infrared answer different questions
Aromatic side chains absorb ultraviolet light. Tryptophan and tyrosine commonly contribute strongly near 280 nm; phenylalanine absorbs more weakly with a shorter-wavelength maximum. A normalised spectrum compares shapes after rescaling and cannot, by itself, compare absolute absorptivities. The source graph is normalised, so equally tall plotted peaks do not mean equal molar absorbance.

Ultraviolet absorption involves electronic transitions. Ordinary mid-infrared absorption mainly probes molecular vibrations, including bond stretching and bending, rather than being the same electronic excitation mechanism. The lecturer wording grouping UV and infrared electronic excitation is corrected here. Ordinary free unmodified common amino acids are not visibly coloured, but cofactors, chemical modifications or engineered protein chromophores make “no protein can absorb visible light” false.

Fluorescence is emission after excitation and is not identical to absorption. Its intensity depends on residue identity, environment and quenching. An absorbance reading at one wavelength does not uniquely reveal sequence, fold or protein purity.

## Worked theoretical concentration and interpretation
For a hypothetical purified protein, suppose the explicitly supplied molar absorptivity at 280 nm is 10,000 L mol−1 cm−1, the path length is 1 cm and blank-corrected absorbance is 0.50. Beer–Lambert A=εcl gives c=0.50/(10,000×1)=5×10−5 mol/L, or 50 µmol/L. A tenfold dilution means the original sample was 500 µmol/L. These supplied values are not universal constants or measurements from this project. Scattering, nucleic-acid contamination or nonlinearity would invalidate a simple calculation unless addressed.

## Short concept checks
- Why can free proline fail a rule that all amino acids give the same purple ninhydrin signal? **Answer:** Its secondary amino group produces a different yellow product under the ordinary test conditions.
- What is wrong with comparing absolute molar absorptivity from equal-height normalised peaks? **Answer:** Normalisation rescales intensities; absolute response information has been removed.

## Key takeaways
Name the reacting group and the measurement. Detection, identification and quantification are different claims; no wet-lab assay is represented as executed here.
