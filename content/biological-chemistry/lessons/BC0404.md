# Electron domains, molecular shape and polarity

## Learning goals
Use electron-domain counting to predict simple shapes. Distinguish electron-domain geometry, molecular shape and net dipole, including molecules with polar bonds but no net molecular dipole.

## From Lewis structure to shape
VSEPR reasoning arranges regions of valence electron density to reduce repulsion. A single, double or triple bond counts as one region for this local geometry analysis; a lone pair is another region. With two regions, the ideal arrangement is linear; three gives trigonal planar; four gives tetrahedral. The approximation is most useful when the Lewis structure and its domain count are appropriate.

Molecular shape describes the positions of nuclei, while electron-domain geometry includes lone pairs. CH₄ has four bonding regions and a tetrahedral shape, with an ideal angle near 109.5°. NH₃ has three bonding regions and one lone pair: tetrahedral domain geometry but trigonal-pyramidal molecular shape, with angles near 107°. H₂O has two bonding regions and two lone pairs: the same broad domain arrangement but bent molecular shape, near 104.5°. The values are useful examples, not universal angles for every molecule with the same domain count.

Lone pairs are not shared between two bonded nuclei and generally occupy more space around the central atom in this simplified model. Their interactions help compress bond angles relative to an ideal tetrahedron. Repulsion rules organise the explanation, but precise geometry also depends on the actual atoms and bonding.

### Five and six electron domains

Five regions give a trigonal-bipyramidal electron-domain arrangement: three equatorial positions separated by 120° and two axial positions at 90° to the equatorial plane. PCl₅ is the basic five-bond example. In the simple VSEPR treatment, a lone pair tends to favour an equatorial site because that site has fewer 90° contacts. SF₄ with four bonds and one lone pair is seesaw-shaped; ClF₃ with three bonds and two lone pairs is T-shaped; XeF₂ with two bonds and three lone pairs is linear. A linear molecular shape therefore does not uniquely establish a two-domain electronic arrangement.

Six regions give an octahedral electron-domain arrangement. SF₆ is the six-bond example, with adjacent directions at 90°. One lone pair gives a square-pyramidal molecular shape in examples such as BrF₅. Two lone pairs placed opposite one another leave a square-planar molecular shape, as in XeF₄. Coordination diagrams and electron-domain diagrams must not be read as interchangeable without checking the electronic model, especially for transition metals.

The lecturer associates five and six regions with sp³d and sp³d² labels. These are traditional introductory hybridisation labels for the stated geometries, not proof that large d-orbital participation is the physical cause of hypervalent main-group bonding. Modern descriptions require a more careful account. The geometry can be taught and tested without converting that historical mechanism into a literal universal rule.

## Worked polarity comparison
A bond dipole reflects unequal electron sharing. Molecular polarity depends on the vector sum of bond dipoles and the complete charge distribution, so bond polarity alone is insufficient.

CO₂ has two electron domains around carbon and is linear. Its two equivalent C=O bond dipoles point oppositely and cancel by symmetry. Water is bent, so its O–H bond dipoles do not cancel. Both molecules contain polar bonds, but their net dipoles differ.

BF₃ provides a second comparison: three equivalent B–F bonds in a trigonal-planar arrangement have no net dipole in the ideal isolated molecule. ## Biological application and scope
Complementary shape and interactions contribute to molecular recognition. The lock-and-key image is a useful introduction, but enzymes and substrates are dynamic; induced fit and conformational selection improve on a rigid-key picture. Geometry does not alone prove that a substance binds a protein or acts as a drug. Charges, solvation and specific interactions also matter.

## Short concept checks
- Does a carbon–oxygen double bond count as two VSEPR domains? **Answer:** No. It counts as one region of electron density in this analysis.
- Why does CO₂ have no net dipole while H₂O does? **Answer:** The equivalent CO₂ bond dipoles cancel in its linear geometry; the bent water geometry prevents equivalent cancellation.

## Key takeaways
Count domains before naming molecular shape. Include lone pairs in the first step and symmetry in the polarity step; polar bonds need not imply a polar molecule.
