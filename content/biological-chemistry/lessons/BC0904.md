# Compartmentation, transport and multienzyme organisation

## Learning goals
Explain why physical location can regulate metabolism, distinguish a metabolon from a universal enzyme arrangement and qualify cellular-location generalisations.

## Location changes available partners
A compartment contains a particular set of enzymes, substrates, cofactors and physical conditions. Membranes restrict unassisted movement of many charged or polar compounds; transporters help determine which forms cross and at what rates. Thus, two reactions in different compartments need not share the same substrate or redox-carrier pool merely because both are drawn in one textbook pathway.

Compartmentation can separate competing activities, maintain suitable conditions and coordinate transport with chemistry. However, a metabolic process can span several locations. It is too broad to say all enzymes participating in a given process are always confined to one organelle. A transport step can itself be a significant control point and must be included when interpreting a whole-cell rate.

## A qualified eukaryotic glucose map
Glycolytic reactions in the usual animal-cell model occur in the cytosol. Pyruvate entry into mitochondria permits further oxidation through mitochondrial reactions, while the respiratory chain and ATP synthase are associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane. Most citric-cycle reactions occur in the matrix; succinate dehydrogenase is membrane-associated. This is more precise than placing every metabolic step indiscriminately inside “the mitochondrion”.

Some cells or conditions rely on other routes; mature mammalian red blood cells lack mitochondria. Bacteria have no mitochondria and can locate respiratory activities in their plasma membrane. Chloroplasts contain photosynthetic electron-transfer machinery. Nuclear transcription is important, but mitochondrial and chloroplast transcription show why “all RNA polymerases are nuclear” is not universal.

Gluconeogenic and other multistep processes can involve mitochondria, cytosol and endoplasmic reticulum. The source's compartment examples are therefore useful anchors, not rigid single-address rules for every organism and pathway. This also connects the biochemical roles to Cell Structure without changing that course's established IDs or progress.

## Diffusion, complexes and channeling
Sequential soluble enzymes can exchange intermediates through the surrounding medium. In a multienzyme complex, proximity and defined transfer can reduce exposure of unstable intermediates or coordinate successive chemistry. A metabolon is an organised association of pathway enzymes, sometimes dynamic rather than a single permanently fixed assembly.

Not every pathway is a stable complex and not every observed protein association proves direct substrate channeling. Evidence should distinguish genuine transfer from simple co-localisation. The lecturer's membrane example adds another geometry: lateral diffusion and membrane-associated organisation can influence encounters, but a drawing of adjacent enzymes is not proof that every substrate moves only in two dimensions.

## Worked transport constraint
Suppose a hypothetical cytosolic precursor is supplied at 8 µmol/min but a required transporter can deliver only 3 µmol/min under the stated conditions. Even a downstream enzyme with isolated capacity 20 µmol/min cannot sustain a steady conversion of 20 µmol/min from that sole input. In the simplest one-to-one no-other-source model, transport bounds sustained supply at 3 µmol/min. Increasing that enzyme alone need not increase flux.

This is a conditional supply example, not a universal assertion that one smallest-capacity number always determines every network flux. Pools, alternate routes, feedback and changing transport conditions can alter the behaviour. Explicit boundaries are essential for interpreting compartment-level experiments.

## Short concept checks
- Does cytosolic glycolysis require every later glucose-oxidation reaction to be cytosolic too? **Answer:** No. Linked processes can occupy different compartments and require transport between them.
- Does co-localisation alone prove substrate channeling? **Answer:** No. Direct transfer requires evidence beyond merely observing associated enzymes.

## Key takeaways
A location is part of the mechanism. Include selective transport, available pools, organism and multienzyme evidence when explaining a pathway.
