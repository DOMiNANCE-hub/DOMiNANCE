# Active sites, specificity and catalytic mechanisms

## Learning goals
Relate recognition to active-site chemistry, compare lock-and-key and induced-fit descriptions and explain how catalytic groups can help a reaction proceed.

## Recognition must support a productive reaction
An active site is the region where substrates bind and catalytic chemistry occurs. Residues distant in the primary sequence can come together in the folded protein to form it. Hydrogen bonds, electrostatic contacts, hydrophobic effects and shape complementarity help select suitable partners. Binding alone is insufficient: a molecule may bind tightly without being transformed, and an inhibitor may occupy a productive site while preventing turnover.

Specificity describes preferential reactions with suitable substrates, including distinctions between related groups or stereoisomers. Some enzymes have narrow substrate ranges; others transform several related compounds. The slides themselves compare glucokinase and broader hexokinase activity, so their separate absolute “no side reactions” statement must not become a universal assessment rule. Substrate concentration, competing molecules and reaction conditions influence observed selectivity.

## Flexibility improves the recognition model
The lock-and-key analogy captures complementary recognition but makes the site seem rigid. Induced fit describes conformational changes associated with ligand binding. These can improve positioning of reactive groups, exclude inappropriate solvent or coordinate successive catalytic steps. Enzymes also sample conformations before binding; induced fit is a useful model, not proof that every motion starts only after a substrate arrives.

For a kinase acting on a sugar, binding can help bring the sugar hydroxyl and a phosphate donor into a productive arrangement. A molecule that merely resembles part of the sugar may fail to induce or sustain the required geometry. Strongest possible binding to the unreacted ground state would not automatically maximise catalysis: the reaction must reach the transition state and the products must eventually leave.

## Why mechanisms work
Proximity and orientation can bring reacting groups together while reducing unproductive arrangements. General acid–base catalysis transfers protons at useful steps, helping a nucleophile react or a leaving group depart. The protonation state of a catalytic residue therefore matters; a group cannot both retain and donate the same proton in the same elementary step.

Covalent catalysis temporarily links the enzyme to a reaction intermediate through a suitable nucleophilic group. Subsequent chemistry releases product and restores the enzyme. This differs from irreversible covalent inhibition, where a sufficiently persistent adduct disables turnover. Metal-ion catalysis can stabilise charges, orient a substrate or help activate water. Several strategies can operate together, and listing one strategy does not establish a complete atomic mechanism for an unstudied enzyme.

## Worked distinction between binding and turnover
Suppose analogue A binds strongly but produces no detectable product, while substrate B binds less tightly and undergoes many catalytic cycles. The observations do not justify calling A the better substrate solely because of binding strength. A may be an inhibitor or may require another condition. Compare product-formation rates, substrate concentrations and appropriate kinetic constants under controlled conditions.

Similarly, stereoselectivity can arise because a three-dimensional active site positions one stereoisomer's groups productively while another's cannot align all necessary contacts. Equal molecular formulae do not ensure identical enzyme recognition. This connects Unit 4 stereochemistry and Unit 7 side-chain chemistry to functional catalysis.

## Short concept checks
- Must two active-site residues be adjacent in the amino-acid sequence? **Answer:** No. Folding can bring distant sequence positions together.
- Is a temporary enzyme–substrate covalent intermediate automatically irreversible inhibition? **Answer:** No. Productive covalent catalysis includes steps that regenerate the enzyme.

## Key takeaways
Recognition, productive geometry, chemical transformation and product release are separate parts of catalysis. Mechanistic explanations require more than a rigid shape analogy.
