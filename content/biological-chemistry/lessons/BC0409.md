# Geometric isomerism and E/Z assignment

## Learning goals
Recognise when alkene E/Z isomerism is possible. Apply Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priorities independently at each double-bond carbon and distinguish geometric labels from predictions of stability.

## Restricted rotation plus different substituents
An ordinary C=C bond restricts rotation because rotation disrupts pi overlap. To obtain distinct E/Z arrangements, each alkene carbon must also have two different substituents. CH₂=CH₂ and CH₂=CHCl do not satisfy this condition at both carbons. But-2-ene does, because each carbon has H and CH₃.

Cis and trans describe relative positions of suitable corresponding groups: same side versus opposite side in familiar examples. E/Z is more general and uses priority rather than visual bulk. It does not require all four substituents around the double bond to be different from one another; it requires a difference within each carbon's own pair.

## Worked priority method
1. Treat the substituent pair attached to the left alkene carbon separately from the pair on the right.
2. Compare the directly attached atoms by atomic number. Higher atomic number means higher priority. For isotopes of the same element, higher mass number takes priority.
3. If the first atoms tie, compare their attached-atom lists in descending priority until the first difference. Multiple bonds are handled with the standard duplicate-atom convention, not by counting bond lines as extra real substituents.
4. Locate the higher-priority substituent on each carbon. If these two are on the same side, assign Z; on opposite sides, assign E.

For but-2-ene, CH₃ outranks H at both carbons because carbon outranks hydrogen. The same-side CH₃ arrangement is Z and the opposite-side arrangement E.

Now suppose the left carbon bears Br above and I below, while the right bears Cl above and F below. I outranks Br on the left; Cl outranks F on the right. The selected groups are below-left and above-right: opposite sides, so E. Do not compare I with Cl across the entire molecule and choose only one “highest” group. Two independent local comparisons are required.

If a carbon bears ethyl and methyl, both begin with carbon. Compare the next layer: ethyl's first carbon is attached to C,H,H, while methyl's is attached to H,H,H. Ethyl therefore has higher priority. Atomic number, rather than alphabetical order or total group mass, determines this comparison.

## Stability, rings and naming scope
A trans arrangement often reduces crowding for simple alkenes, but E or trans is not a universal guarantee of greater stability. Other interactions can favour different arrangements. E/Z is a structural assignment, not a thermodynamic measurement.

Rings can also sustain same-face and opposite-face substituent arrangements. A flat ring sketch needs explicit up/down or wedge/dash information; placing a label to the left on paper does not establish that it lies below the ring plane. Full systematic numbering is separate from CIP assignment. The lecturer's suggestion that the highest-priority atom must always get the lowest name locant is not a general nomenclature rule and is not used as an answer key here.

## Short concept checks
- Can CH₂=C(Cl)Br have E/Z isomers? **Answer:** No. One alkene carbon has two identical hydrogen substituents.
- In the Br/I versus Cl/F example, which groups determine E or Z? **Answer:** I on its carbon and Cl on the other; their relative sides determine the descriptor.

## Key takeaways
Check eligibility first, then rank each carbon's pair and compare the selected sides. Priority, naming order, visual size and thermodynamic stability are distinct questions.
