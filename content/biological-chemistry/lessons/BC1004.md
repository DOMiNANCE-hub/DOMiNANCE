# Essential fatty acids, modified chains and lipid mediators

## Learning goals
Distinguish the two routinely essential human fatty acids, reconcile the α/γ-linolenic discrepancy and explain precursor roles without converting them into clinical prescriptions.

## Essential means synthesis cannot meet the required supply
In the usual human nutritional classification, linoleic acid, 18:2n-6, and α-linolenic acid, 18:3n-3, must be obtained from the diet. Human synthetic pathways do not generate their required parent double-bond patterns from saturated chains. This is a statement about synthetic capability and supply, not a claim that all other fatty acids are biologically unimportant.

Actual Lecture 10 p.10 names γ-linolenic acid in the essential pair, while actual p.11 labels ALA in its omega-3 structure figure. The available NIH Office of Dietary Supplements introduction independently identifies linoleic acid and ALA as essential. The teaching pair here therefore uses **α-linolenic**, with the discrepancy retained in the review ledger rather than repeated in a scored key.

γ-Linolenic acid is a different n-6 molecule, not an alternative spelling of ALA. Its Δ6,9,12 positions differ from ALA's Δ9,12,15. It can arise in metabolism from linoleic acid through desaturation; the introductory human-essential pair must not be changed merely because both names contain linolenic.

## Elongation and desaturation connect families
Enzymes can elongate chains and modify double-bond patterns within the pathways available to the organism. Arachidonic acid is a common 20:4n-6 example related metabolically to linoleic acid. It can also be obtained from foods; saying it can only exist through mammalian synthesis is too absolute. Organism, life stage and metabolic capacity affect requirements and products.

ALA can contribute to longer-chain n-3 products such as EPA and DHA, but conversion is limited and variable. This lesson does not prescribe a conversion percentage, a supplement dose or a universal dietary intervention. EPA and DHA also occur through food-chain sources, including microalgal production and accumulation in marine organisms.

## Precursors support local signalling chemistry
Eicosanoids are signalling molecules commonly derived from twenty-carbon polyunsaturated precursors. Arachidonic-acid pathways provide examples including prostaglandins and related mediators; EPA provides other precursor possibilities. The product depends on enzyme pathway, cell type and context, not simply on the presence of any unsaturated bond.

A signalling precursor is not the same as a hormone itself, and not every lipid signal is an eicosanoid. Steroid hormones, ether-lipid mediators and membrane-derived second messengers use distinct chemical structures. Broad biological relevance does not by itself justify a clinical diagnosis or a claim that one dietary lipid universally cures inflammation.

## Modified chains illustrate diversity
The source includes branched and cyclic microbial fatty acids. A branch can perturb packing locally, while a cyclic group changes geometry in another way. Such structures remain part of lipid diversity even though a simple saturated/unsaturated count does not describe them completely. Their occurrence should be linked to a verified organism and structure rather than converted into an unsupported universal biomarker.

## Worked distinction between identity and category
If two samples are both labelled 18:3, their exact structures remain unresolved. Adding n-3 versus n-6 separates ALA-like and γ-linolenic-like families, and complete delta/geometry notation can establish the detailed species. The conclusion “both must be the same essential fatty acid” would ignore missing positional information.

## Short concept checks
- Which linolenic form belongs in the routinely essential human pair with linoleic acid? **Answer:** α-Linolenic acid, ALA, an n-3 fatty acid.
- Does 18:3 alone prove a sample is ALA? **Answer:** No. Position and geometry are not fully specified by carbon and double-bond counts alone.

## Key takeaways
Resolve α/γ identity before assessing nutritional classification. Precursor relationships explain chemistry; they do not supply individual treatment advice.
