# Structural, microbial and other important glycans

## Learning goals
Relate glycosidic geometry to structural strength and compare cellulose, chitin, peptidoglycan, lipopolysaccharide and selected algal glycans. Preserve organism and matrix-specific qualifications.

## Cellulose versus storage glucans
Cellulose is a β(1→4)-linked glucose polymer. Its stereochemistry supports extended chains and interchain interactions in strong fibrillar structures, unlike the helical/branched α-linked storage examples. Hydrogen bonding helps organise chains, but the β glycosidic bonds are covalent; do not describe the entire polymer as held together only by hydrogen bonds.

Humans lack the enzyme system needed to hydrolyse cellulose efficiently into absorbable glucose. It contributes dietary fibre rather than functioning exactly like digested starch. Ruminants obtain products of microbial cellulose fermentation; the animal's ability to use plant material does not prove that its own tissues synthesise every necessary cellulase. Cellulose chain lengths vary, so a single slide count is not a universal molecular size.

Chitin contains β(1→4)-linked N-acetylglucosamine and supports fungal walls and arthropod exoskeletons. It is not simply glucose cellulose with an unrelated protein stuck to the outside. Compare the repeating residue as well as the linkage: cellulose and chitin share β(1→4) topology but differ in monomer chemistry.

## Bacterial envelopes contain distinct glycoconjugates
Peptidoglycan has alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid sugars linked into glycan chains, with peptide connections providing additional reinforcement. The short peptide structures vary among organisms; a lysine-containing illustrative stem should not be treated as an exact universal bacterial sequence. Many bacteria use peptidoglycan, whereas archaea do not have bacterial peptidoglycan.

Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide, with lipid A, core carbohydrate and, in many forms, O-antigen chains. This is a lipid-associated carbohydrate structure distinct from the underlying peptidoglycan layer. Lipid A is important to endotoxin activity; it is not sound to assign the same biological effect to every isolated sugar or all bacterial walls. LPS is not a feature of every Gram-positive envelope.

## Other source examples and naming limits
Dextran is a glucose polymer commonly described here with an α(1→6) backbone and variable branch linkages. Cross-linked dextran can form a porous separation matrix; the slide juxtaposes Bio-Gel and Sephadex labels, so a commercial brand identity is not assessed from that figure. Matrix chemistry and size-exclusion principle are more reliable teaching targets than the inconsistent label.

Alginates contain mannuronate and guluronate residues and interact with calcium; agarose contains alternating galactose-derived residues and forms gels. These illustrate how charge, sequence and conformation change macromolecular properties. Galacturonans contain galacturonic acid, whereas galactans contain galactose: the similar names do not erase the carboxyl group.

## Short concept checks
- Why is cellulose resistant to human α-amylase? **Answer:** It has β(1→4) rather than the suitable α(1→4) linkages recognised by that enzyme.
- Is Gram-negative LPS the same molecule as peptidoglycan? **Answer:** No. LPS is lipid-associated material in the outer membrane; peptidoglycan is a distinct glycan–peptide network.

## Key takeaways
Structural glycans combine residue chemistry, covalent linkages and higher-order organisation. Organism labels and variable examples need to be retained when making a comparison.
