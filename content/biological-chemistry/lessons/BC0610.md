# Modified bases, purine products and therapeutic analogues

## Learning goals
Distinguish natural base modification, catabolism and structural analogues. Explain representative drug mechanisms through verified chemistry without assuming all analogues act identically or offering treatment recommendations.

## Modification is not automatically damage
The source illustrates methylated and other modified bases. Enzymatic methylation can affect recognition and regulation while retaining a base's parent relationship; many tRNAs contain modified residues important to folding or decoding. A modified base is not automatically a mutation, and a modification pattern does not alone reveal its physiological effect.

Base, nucleoside and nucleotide analogues are also different categories. A base analogue may need metabolic conversion before affecting a pathway. A nucleoside analogue contains a sugar-like component but may not yet bear its active phosphate groups. Structural similarity can permit binding or incorporation while a small difference changes a subsequent reaction.

## Purine breakdown and urate
Adenine deamination gives hypoxanthine; guanine deamination gives xanthine in the source comparison. Hypoxanthine can be oxidised to xanthine and then to urate; guanine deamination enters at xanthine. The linked steps should not be read as one identical direct oxidation for every starting base. Humans lack functional urate oxidase, consistent with the Cell Structure qualification, so this example must not assume normal human conversion of urate to allantoin by that enzyme.

Monosodium urate crystal deposition can trigger inflammation in gout, but an elevated urate measurement does not by itself prove that a person has gout. A base-degradation pathway is not a diagnostic rule or a reason to prescribe a drug. Caffeine, theobromine and theophylline are methylxanthine-related compounds; they are not complete nucleic-acid polymers or all interchangeable nucleotide chain terminators.

## Two mechanisms show why analogues differ
Zidovudine (AZT) is a thymidine-related nucleoside analogue with an azido substitution at the 3′ position. Intracellular phosphorylation produces the active triphosphate form. Incorporation by viral reverse transcriptase can terminate normal extension because the required ordinary 3′ OH is absent. The structural lesson connects substrate recognition, activation and the next chemical step; it does not imply universal selectivity or a complete treatment regimen.

5-Fluorouracil is a fluoropyrimidine base analogue with several active metabolic routes. One metabolite, FdUMP, inhibits thymidylate synthase and affects thymidine-nucleotide supply; other metabolites can affect RNA/DNA processes. It should not be reduced to the statement that every analogue is a directly incorporated 3′-deoxy chain terminator. The lecturer's analogue figure includes both natural comparators and drugs, so proximity in the figure does not make every displayed molecule a medicine.

These representative mechanisms are checked against the labelled source structures and independent drug descriptions. Appropriate clinical use, dosing, toxicity and combination choices require clinical sources and are not assessed from this introductory structural comparison.

## Short concept checks
- Why is intracellular phosphorylation important to the AZT example? **Answer:** The nucleoside is converted to an active triphosphate form used in the polymerase-related mechanism.
- Must every base analogue inhibit nucleic-acid synthesis by exactly the same chain-termination mechanism? **Answer:** No. Different analogues and metabolites can affect enzymes, substrate supply, incorporation or RNA function through distinct mechanisms.

## Key takeaways
Track the chemical form that acts and the reaction it changes. Natural modification, degradation products and therapeutic analogues have different biological meanings.
