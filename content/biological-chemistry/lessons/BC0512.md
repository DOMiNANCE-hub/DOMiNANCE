# Glycoproteins, proteoglycans and extracellular function

## Learning goals
Distinguish carbohydrate attachment to proteins from the structure of a free polysaccharide. Explain N/O linkage, glycosaminoglycan-rich proteoglycans and how charged chains support hydrated matrices.

## Protein plus specific carbohydrate attachment
A glycoprotein has covalently attached carbohydrate chains. Glycans can affect folding, stability, recognition, targeting or interactions; they are not merely interchangeable energy stores. Examples include receptors, transport proteins and immunoglobulins. The protein's amino-acid sequence and the glycan's residue/linkage pattern provide different kinds of information.

In the common N-linked context, attachment involves the side-chain amide nitrogen of asparagine. Common O-linked attachment uses a hydroxyl side chain, particularly serine or threonine; specialised contexts can involve other hydroxylated residues. N-linked does not mean attached to any backbone nitrogen, and O-linked does not mean every sugar oxygen bonds indiscriminately to a protein.

## Proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans
Proteoglycans have core proteins carrying glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains, often long repeating disaccharide polymers. Many GAGs have carboxylate and/or sulfate groups that confer substantial negative charge. Their association with counterions and water promotes hydration and helps extracellular matrices resist compression and recover shape. Protein organisation and interactions also matter; charge alone does not explain every tissue property.

Many common GAG chains attach through a sugar linker to a serine residue. The lecture's statement that all GAGs are O-linked to Ser-Gly is too universal. Keratan sulfate can have different N- or O-linked associations depending on type, and hyaluronan is a major nonsulfated GAG not covalently attached to a core protein in the same way. Aggrecan can associate noncovalently with hyaluronan, so an aggregate is not evidence that every visible chain is covalently continuous.

## Worked functional distinction
Compare a small branched glycan on a signalling receptor with an extracellular core protein carrying many long charged GAG chains. Both involve carbohydrate–protein relationships, but their chain architecture and dominant roles differ. The receptor glycan can influence recognition; the GAG-rich matrix can retain water and distribute compression. The distinction is useful without imposing an arbitrary percentage cutoff that the source does not establish as a universal definition.

A carbohydrate group on a membrane protein also does not imply it faces the cytosol. Secretory-pathway orientation generally presents cell-surface glycans to the extracellular space, connecting this lesson with Cell Structure's glycocalyx. Detailed glycan biosynthesis and every GAG exception require further source-specific review; this unit introduces the explicit attachment and function principles.

## Short concept checks
- Does N-linked identify attachment to any nitrogen atom on a protein? **Answer:** No. The common pathway described links through an asparagine side-chain amide nitrogen.
- Why can a GAG-rich matrix retain water? **Answer:** Many chains contain negatively charged groups that associate with counterions and water within an organised extracellular network.

## Key takeaways
Specify core protein, chain type and linkage. Glycoprotein, proteoglycan, GAG and noncovalent aggregate are related terms with different meanings and important exceptions.
