# Triacylglycerols, waxes and concentrated energy storage

## Learning goals
Identify ester counts and backbones, compare simple/mixed triacylglycerols and waxes, and perform a supplied mass/energy calculation without confusing chemical energy with cellular ATP yield.

## Three acyl groups on glycerol
Glycerol contains three hydroxyl groups. Esterifying all three with fatty acids gives a triacylglycerol, often called a triglyceride. Each ester involves a glycerol oxygen linked to a fatty-acyl carbonyl carbon. The chains can be identical, giving a simple triacylglycerol, or different, giving a mixed one. Most natural fats are mixtures rather than a single simple compound.

Triacylglycerols are largely neutral and hydrophobic. Their esterified fatty acids do not each carry the free carboxylate charge of an isolated fatty acid. Mono- and diacylglycerols retain one or more free glycerol OH groups and can have different interfacial or signalling behaviour; they are not merely smaller droplets with every property identical to a triacylglycerol.

## Waxes use a different alcohol backbone
A typical wax ester joins a long-chain fatty acid to a long-chain monohydric alcohol, giving one ester connection in that simple molecule. It is not a glycerol triester. Wax-rich surface mixtures can provide water repellency and protection, with examples such as plant surface waxes and wool wax. Composition varies, so cosmetic/pharmaceutical uses are not proof that every wax has an identical structure or skin behaviour.

A cholesteryl ester is another fatty-acid ester, with cholesterol's OH esterified. It differs from free cholesterol, which retains that OH and is more amphipathic. Structural classification should therefore distinguish free sterol, sterol ester, wax and acylglycerol rather than grouping them solely by water insolubility.

## Worked formal esterification mass
Using explicitly supplied rounded free molar masses glycerol=92, fatty acid=256 and water=18 g/mol, the formal mass of a simple glycerol triester is 92+3×256−3×18=806 g/mol. Three ester-forming condensations remove three waters in this bookkeeping representation. A table of acyl-residue masses would require different handling; do not subtract the same lost groups twice.

This balance is not a claim that cells synthesise stored fat by mixing free glycerol and acids without activation. Biological acyl transfer uses activated substrates and enzymes. The formal atom balance and the actual biosynthetic mechanism answer different questions, as in peptide formation.

## Why storage mass can be efficient
The lecturer gives approximate energy comparisons of 38 kJ/g for triacylglycerol versus 17 kJ/g for carbohydrate/protein. The relatively reduced carbon and relatively low associated water make triacylglycerol a compact reserve. Adipose tissue additionally supports insulation and mechanical cushioning; it is a functioning tissue, not only an inert energy container.

For a hypothetical comparison using those supplied values, 10 g of triacylglycerol corresponds to approximately 380 kJ. Matching that energy at 17 kJ/g requires 380/17≈22.4 g of the comparator before considering associated water. This is a teaching comparison, not a measured calorimetry result, a prescription or a universal ATP yield. Digestion, absorption and metabolism add further context.

## Short concept checks
- How many ordinary fatty-acid ester connections are in one triacylglycerol? **Answer:** Three, one at each glycerol hydroxyl position in the fully esterified molecule.
- Why is a wax ester not simply a triacylglycerol with a different name? **Answer:** Its typical alcohol is long-chain and monohydric, giving a different backbone and one ester rather than glycerol's three.

## Key takeaways
Count linkages from actual structure. Neutral storage, amphipathic interfaces and energy comparisons require different chemical explanations.
