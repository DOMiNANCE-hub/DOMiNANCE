# Ionisation energies and meaningful exceptions

## Learning goals
Define successive ionisation energies with appropriate gaseous species. Explain broad trends and the Be/B and N/O exceptions, and interpret a large jump in a successive-energy series.

## Removing electrons costs energy
First ionisation energy concerns removing an electron from a gaseous atom in its ground state: X(g) → X+(g) + e−. For molar values, the reported energy refers to one mole of this process. The second ionisation energy removes an electron from X+(g), not from a fresh neutral atom. The third starts from X²+(g). Species and charge must be explicit.

Across a main-group period, increased effective nuclear attraction and generally smaller size tend to raise first ionisation energy. Down a group, greater spatial extent and shielding tend to reduce it. These broad tendencies are not an instruction to draw a perfectly monotonic line through every element. Subshell energy, penetration, occupancy and electron pairing create important exceptions.

Beryllium is 1s² 2s², while boron is 1s² 2s² 2p¹. Boron's electron is removed from the higher-energy, less penetrating 2p subshell, so its first ionisation energy is lower than beryllium's despite its greater nuclear charge. The phrase “higher energy level” here must not be confused with a higher principal n: both 2s and 2p have n = 2.

Nitrogen has 2p³ with three parallel singly occupied p orbitals. Oxygen has 2p⁴ and therefore one pair. Repulsion within that pair contributes to oxygen's lower first ionisation energy. The comparison does not mean oxygen is generally unstable or that every half-filled subshell outranks every other energetic contribution.

## Worked successive-energy interpretation
Consider illustrative first, second and third ionisation energies of 600, 1,800 and 14,000 kJ/mol. The very large increase after the second electron suggests that removing a third reaches a much more tightly held inner-shell population. For a simple main-group ground-state atom, this supports two relatively accessible valence electrons. It does not uniquely identify the element without further information, and the values are an illustrative pattern rather than a copied measured dataset.

For magnesium, [Ne] 3s², the first two removals produce Mg²+ with [Ne]. A third removal disrupts that closed inner configuration and is much more costly. The second removal still costs energy: “noble-gas configuration is favourable” must not be interpreted as an isolated gaseous electron-removal step releasing energy automatically.

## Distinctions and applications
Ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity answer different questions. Ionisation energy measures electron removal from a specified gaseous species. It does not alone determine the overall energy of salt formation, which includes other processes such as electron addition and interactions in the solid. A cell does not choose a metal oxidation state solely from its first gaseous ionisation energy; ligands, solvent and coupled reactions matter.

## Short concept checks
- Why can boron have lower first ionisation energy than beryllium? **Answer:** Boron's removed electron occupies a higher-energy, less penetrating 2p subshell rather than Be's 2s subshell.
- What species loses an electron in a second-ionisation process? **Answer:** A gaseous singly charged cation, X+(g), forming X²+(g).

## Key takeaways
Write the species and charge first. Explain trends through structure, then test for subshell and pairing exceptions. A large successive-energy jump identifies a change in electron population rather than a free-standing universal group-number rule.
