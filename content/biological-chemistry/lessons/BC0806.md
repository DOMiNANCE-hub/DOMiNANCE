# Deriving the simple Michaelis–Menten rate equation

## Learning goals
Explain the steady-state model, distinguish it from rapid binding equilibrium, derive the hyperbolic rate law and state when applying that law is justified.

## A simple model and its assumptions
Consider E+S ⇌ ES → E+P, with association constant k1, dissociation constant k−1 and product-forming constant k2. This is an explicitly simplified mechanism. Real enzymes may have more intermediates, multiple substrates, conformational steps or reversibility; a hyperbolic curve alone does not prove this exact atomic pathway.

Use initial-rate conditions with negligible reverse flux from product, approximately constant available substrate over the measurement interval and fixed active enzyme concentration ET. After a brief transient, the steady-state approximation sets d[ES]/dt≈0: ES forms and disappears at approximately balancing rates. ES need not be at thermodynamic equilibrium with E and S, and the entire reaction can still have a net product flux.

A common experimental regime has substrate in substantial excess over enzyme and negligible depletion in the fitted interval. This assumption must be checked; the equation should not be applied indiscriminately to a tight-binding assay where enzyme substantially removes free substrate. Concentrations in the mechanism refer to available species, not automatically every analytical total in a complex cell.

## From enzyme conservation to the equation
ES formation is k1[E][S]. ES disappearance is (k−1+k2)[ES]. Equating them gives k1[E][S]=(k−1+k2)[ES]. Define Km=(k−1+k2)/k1, with concentration units. Conservation gives ET=[E]+[ES], so substitute [E]=ET−[ES] and rearrange:

Km[ES]=(ET−[ES])[S], hence [ES]=ET[S]/(Km+[S]). Product formation is v0=k2[ES]. With Vmax=k2ET, the resulting equation is v0=Vmax[S]/(Km+[S]). Vmax has the same rate units as v0; Km and [S] must use the same concentration units.

At [S]=Km, v0=Vmax/2. At [S] much below Km, v0≈(Vmax/Km)[S], nearly linear in substrate. At [S] much above Km, v0 approaches Vmax and becomes relatively insensitive to further substrate increases. No finite concentration gives exactly the asymptotic Vmax in the ideal equation.

## Km is not always a binding dissociation constant
For the binding step alone, Kd=k−1/k1. The simple steady-state Km includes k2 as well. Only under an appropriate rapid-equilibrium limit, k2 much less than k−1, does Km≈Kd. Thus a lower Km does not universally prove tighter equilibrium binding, especially when comparing enzymes with different catalytic steps or mechanisms. The operational half-rate meaning holds only when the specified Michaelis–Menten relationship fits.

## Worked distinction between constants
Suppose k1=2×10^6 M−1 s−1, k−1=20 s−1 and k2=80 s−1. Then Km=(20+80)/(2×10^6)=5×10−5 M=50 µM. But Kd=20/(2×10^6)=1×10−5 M=10 µM. The two differ because product formation is not negligible compared with dissociation. Calling the measured 50 µM a direct equilibrium dissociation constant would erase the model's catalytic contribution.

For cooperative or substrate-inhibited data, a simple hyperbola may be inadequate. Report a justified model and its parameters; use an operational half-rate substrate concentration rather than automatically calling every midpoint Km. Parameters also depend on pH, temperature and other specified conditions.

## Short concept checks
- Does steady state mean ES formation and breakdown have both stopped? **Answer:** No. Their rates approximately balance while catalytic flux can continue.
- Why can Km differ from Kd in the supplied simple mechanism? **Answer:** Km includes the product-forming constant k2 as well as dissociation, whereas Kd describes the binding equilibrium alone.

## Key takeaways
Derive the equation with its assumptions. Km is a concentration parameter of a supported kinetic model, not a universal affinity label.
