# Ionic bonding and the whole energy balance

## Learning goals
Explain ionic attraction and charge balance. Use a simple Born–Haber-style energy account without treating electron transfer or an electronegativity cutoff as a complete explanation.

## From atoms to an extended ionic structure
An ionic solid contains oppositely charged ions attracted in an extended arrangement. Sodium chloride is not best pictured as a collection of isolated NaCl molecules: each ion interacts with several neighbours in a lattice. Its formula records the simplest charge-balanced ratio. One Na+ and one Cl− balance overall charge; Mg²+ requires two Cl− ions, giving MgCl₂.

Producing ions from isolated gaseous atoms is only part of forming a solid from the elements. Removing an electron from sodium requires energy. Adding an electron to chlorine can release energy in the explicit energy-change convention. Separating elemental material into gaseous atoms also has an energetic cost. Bringing gaseous ions together into a crystal releases lattice-formation energy. The complete balance, rather than one favourable step, explains whether the overall formation enthalpy is negative.

Hess's law allows enthalpy changes to be added because enthalpy is a state function: the net change depends on the specified initial and final states. A Born–Haber cycle relates a direct formation process to a sequence of atomisation, ionisation, electron addition and lattice formation. Write every process with its states and stoichiometric amounts before adding numbers. Half a mole of a diatomic reactant requires the corresponding fraction of its dissociation enthalpy.

## Worked illustrative cycle
For an explicitly illustrative monovalent salt, suppose metal atomisation costs +100 kJ/mol, half the halogen dissociation costs +120, metal ionisation costs +500, electron addition contributes −350 and lattice formation contributes −750. Their sum is 100 + 120 + 500 − 350 − 750 = −380 kJ/mol. The overall process is exothermic even though several necessary steps require energy. These supplied values are a reasoning exercise, not measured NaCl data.

If a table instead reports lattice dissociation energy as +750, reverse its sign for lattice formation before summing. Adding both +750 and −750 as separate real steps would double-count a reversal. The electron-affinity sign convention also needs checking: “energy released” may be tabulated positive even when the process energy change is negative.

## Predictive limits
A large electronegativity difference often indicates substantial ionic character, but 1.7 is a teaching heuristic, not a universal boundary at which electrons suddenly become completely transferred. Ionic and covalent descriptions emphasise different features of electron distribution; real bonding can contain mixed character. Ion charge, size, polarisation and structure matter.

A strong lattice does not imply high water solubility. Dissolution also involves solvent reorganisation, hydration and entropy; some ionic compounds dissolve readily while others are sparingly soluble. Conversely, a negative formation enthalpy alone does not establish that every preparation is spontaneous or fast under every condition. Reaction free energy and kinetics address further questions.

## Short concept checks
- Why does MgCl₂ require two chloride ions per magnesium ion? **Answer:** One Mg²+ requires two Cl− ions to give zero net charge.
- In the supplied cycle, what is the sum excluding lattice formation? **Answer:** +370 kJ/mol; including −750 lattice formation gives −380 kJ/mol overall.

## Key takeaways
Explain the extended ionic structure, balance charge and account for every energy step. Sign conventions and initial/final states matter more than memorising a single electronegativity threshold.
