# Working with saturation data and reciprocal plots

## Learning goals
Use the Michaelis–Menten equation in both directions, check units and limiting behaviour, and interpret reciprocal plots without treating them as proof of a unique mechanism.

## Predict a rate with consistent units
For a hypothetical enzyme, let Vmax=120 µM/min and Km=20 µM. At [S]=5 µM, v0=120×5/(20+5)=24 µM/min. At 20 µM the rate is 60; at 80 µM it is 96. A fourfold increase from 20 to 80 µM therefore increases rate only 1.6-fold, because the system moves towards saturation.

At 180 µM, the predicted rate is 120×180/200=108 µM/min, or 90% of Vmax. In general a desired fraction f=v0/Vmax gives [S]=fKm/(1−f). Half-rate requires Km, 90% requires 9Km and 99% requires 99Km. Vmax is approached gradually rather than reached whenever [S] merely exceeds Km.

## Rearranging from a measured rate
Starting from v0(Km+[S])=Vmax[S], isolate [S] to obtain [S]=v0Km/(Vmax−v0). For v0=90 µM/min with the same constants, [S]=90×20/(120−90)=60 µM. A supplied rate greater than Vmax would give a negative result; that is evidence that the assumed constants, conditions, units or model do not describe the observation, not a physically negative substrate concentration.

Alternatively, Km=[S](Vmax/v0−1). At [S]=10 µM, v0=40 µM/min and Vmax=120 µM/min, Km=10×(3−1)=20 µM. This uses an independently specified limiting rate. One arbitrary rate/substrate pair cannot identify both unknown Km and Vmax without additional information.

## A simple table reveals the shape
| [S], µM | v0, µM/min for supplied Vmax 120 and Km 20 |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 |
| 5 | 24 |
| 20 | 60 |
| 60 | 90 |
| 180 | 108 |

These are calculated values, not laboratory measurements. A graph based on them must not display invented experimental uncertainty bars. For actual fitting, collect an informative substrate range, replicates and uncertainties rather than assuming that one apparently flat high-substrate point establishes a true Vmax.

## Reciprocal plotting and its limits
Taking reciprocals yields 1/v0=(Km/Vmax)(1/[S])+1/Vmax. In a Lineweaver–Burk plot, slope is Km/Vmax, y-intercept 1/Vmax and extrapolated x-intercept −1/Km. For the example, y-intercept=1/120 min/µM and slope=20/120 min. The negative x-intercept is an extrapolation, not a physical requirement to prepare negative substrate.

At zero substrate the reciprocal is undefined; do not plot 1/0. Low-rate measurement errors can be magnified by reciprocation, so visual straightness and intersecting lines are not definitive mechanistic evidence. Direct nonlinear fitting of appropriately weighted original rate data is often more useful. A transformed plot can still explain ideal parameter relationships and help check units or gross inconsistencies.

## Comparing enzyme amounts
If active enzyme concentration doubles while all model conditions remain valid, Vmax doubles to 240 µM/min and Km remains 20 µM for the same enzyme/substrate conditions. At [S]=20 µM the rate becomes 120, still half of the new Vmax. This differs from adding substrate or changing the enzyme's catalytic mechanism.

## Short concept checks
- Why does [S]=9Km give 90% rather than 100% of Vmax? **Answer:** The fraction is 9Km/(Km+9Km)=9/10; the ideal asymptote requires unbounded substrate.
- Can one rate/substrate pair determine both Km and Vmax without further information? **Answer:** No. Multiple parameter combinations can explain one pair.

## Key takeaways
Check units and the low/high-substrate limits. Calculations and plots describe a model; model fitting and mechanistic verification remain different tasks.
