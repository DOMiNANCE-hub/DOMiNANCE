# Coordinate bonding and Lewis donor–acceptor chemistry

## Learning goals
Identify the donor and acceptor of an electron pair. Explain NH₄+, H₃O+ and the NH₃–BF₃ adduct without treating a coordinate bond as permanently distinguishable by its origin.

## An electron-pair description
In a coordinate or dative-bond formation description, one participant supplies both electrons of the shared pair. A Lewis base donates an electron pair, while a Lewis acid accepts one. These terms describe electron-pair behaviour rather than requiring a particular solvent or an initial positive charge on every acceptor.

Ammonia has a nitrogen lone pair. A proton can accept that pair to form NH₄+. Water can donate an oxygen lone pair to a proton to form H₃O+. Electron accounting is essential: NH₄+ has four N–H bonds and no remaining nitrogen lone pair in its simple Lewis structure; H₃O+ has three O–H bonds and one oxygen lone pair. The proton does not donate a pair that it does not possess.

An arrow used in a formation drawing points from the electron-pair donor toward the acceptor. Once ammonium forms, its equivalent N–H bonds are not divided into three normal bonds plus one permanently identifiable weaker bond. The arrow records the formation description; the final electronic structure determines properties.

## Worked ammonia–boron trifluoride example
BF₃ has an electron-deficient boron centre in the simple Lewis picture. NH₃ has an available nitrogen lone pair. Nitrogen donates toward boron, forming H₃N→BF₃. In the basic formal-charge accounting, nitrogen now has four bonds and receives formal charge +1, while boron has four bonds and receives −1; the adduct is neutral overall. These formal charges must not be confused with a complete measured map of partial charges.

To identify the donor in an unfamiliar proposal, locate an available pair and a suitable acceptor state. Merely seeing nitrogen is insufficient: protonated NH₄+ has no nitrogen lone pair to donate in this simple picture. An atom's charge, bonding and surrounding groups alter the reasoning.

## Biological coordination
Metal-binding ligands can supply electron pairs through donor atoms such as nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur. The haem group in haemoglobin coordinates iron through porphyrin nitrogens; the protein environment and additional ligands contribute to its chemistry. Coordination number counts donor atoms directly attached to a metal, not the total number of atoms in a ligand. A ligand can sometimes attach through more than one donor atom, producing chelation.

This introduces a mechanistic vocabulary rather than asserting that all metal–ligand bonds have identical strength, that every nitrogen binds every metal or that a simple gas-phase ion describes haem completely. Iron oxidation state, ligand geometry and the protein environment matter; oxygen-binding details belong in appropriately supported later teaching.

## Common traps
Lewis donor–acceptor behaviour is not the same as a complete electron-transfer redox process. Sharing a donated pair differs from transferring a single electron to change oxidation states. A species may participate in acid–base chemistry in one setting and redox chemistry in another; identify the actual process.

## Short concept checks
- Which participant donates the pair when NH₃ binds BF₃? **Answer:** Nitrogen in NH₃ donates its lone pair to the electron-deficient boron centre.
- Can the four equivalent N–H bonds of NH₄+ be labelled permanently by which was “originally dative”? **Answer:** No. The formation description does not make one equivalent bond intrinsically distinct in the final ion.

## Key takeaways
Identify an actual pair donor and suitable acceptor, conserve electrons and distinguish formation arrows from final structure. The same vocabulary helps describe biologically important metal coordination.
