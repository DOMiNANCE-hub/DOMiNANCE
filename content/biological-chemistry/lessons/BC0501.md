# Carbohydrate chemistry, classification and biological roles

## Learning goals
Classify a carbohydrate by its functional groups and number of sugar units. Relate carbohydrate structure to energy supply, storage, support and recognition, while distinguishing molecular chemistry from dietary generalisations.

## What counts as a carbohydrate
Carbohydrates include polyhydroxy aldehydes, polyhydroxy ketones and their derivatives. Polyhydroxy means several hydroxyl groups; a carbonyl group contains C=O. An aldose has an aldehyde in its open-chain form, while a ketose has a ketone. Many sugars predominantly form rings, so failure to see a free C=O in a ring drawing does not exclude the carbohydrate family. Derivatives include deoxy, amino, oxidised, reduced and phosphate-bearing sugars.

The familiar formula (CH₂O)n describes many unmodified monosaccharides. It is a pattern, not a sufficient identification test or a universal rule. Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆, but 2-deoxyribose is C₅H₁₀O₄ and amino sugars contain nitrogen. Conversely, acetic acid, C₂H₄O₂, fits a 1:2:1 atom ratio without being a monosaccharide. Count atoms and inspect connectivity rather than identifying a class from a ratio alone.

A monosaccharide cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrate units. Disaccharides contain two units joined by a glycosidic linkage. This lecture uses oligosaccharide for 2–10 units and polysaccharide for longer chains; conventions at the boundary vary. A free monosaccharide and a sugar residue within a polymer are related forms, not identical chemical formulas.

## Complementary naming systems
Combine the open-chain functional group and carbon count: glyceraldehyde is an aldotriose, ribose an aldopentose, glucose an aldohexose and fructose a ketohexose. Dihydroxyacetone is a ketotriose and has no stereocentre. Triose, tetrose, pentose, hexose and heptose indicate three through seven carbons, which are common introductory examples rather than an upper limit on every sugar derivative in biology. D/L adds a stereochemical relationship and does not count carbons or measure optical rotation.

## Roles and a worked energy calculation
Glucose supplies carbon and energy through regulated metabolism. Glycogen and starch provide mobilisable glucose stores. Cellulose and chitin support structures; ribose and deoxyribose enter nucleic acids; surface glycans contribute to recognition and extracellular organisation. Carbohydrates are important stores, but they are not the only or universally predominant stored energy in animals: lipid stores are also important.

Using the lecture's approximate nutritional factor of 4 kcal per gram of digestible carbohydrate, 30 g contributes about 120 kcal. In an illustrative 2,000-kcal intake that is 120/2,000×100=6%. This calculation neither assigns cellulose 4 kcal/g of directly available glucose nor establishes a person's dietary requirement. Fibre digestion and microbial fermentation differ from absorption of free sugars.

The slide's simple-versus-complex dietary comparisons are broad context. Food processing, fibre, portion and total diet affect outcomes; a polysaccharide is not automatically a healthier food in every preparation. Numerical diet and blood-glucose ranges in the slides are not adopted here as universal clinical or institutional guidance.

## Short concept checks
- Does C₅H₁₀O₄ automatically exclude a molecule from carbohydrate chemistry? **Answer:** No. 2-Deoxyribose is a carbohydrate derivative with one fewer oxygen than ribose.
- A 15-g digestible carbohydrate portion uses the stated 4-kcal/g factor. What energy estimate follows? **Answer:** Approximately 60 kcal, under that explicit nutritional assumption.

## Key takeaways
Use functional groups, connectivity, stereochemistry and unit count together. Formula patterns and dietary averages have defined limits; biological roles extend far beyond sweetness or fuel.
