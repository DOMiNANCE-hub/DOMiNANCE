# Recognising the chemistry inside pathway arrows

## Learning goals
Explain the lecturer's six reaction examples, preserve atom/electron balances and distinguish an overall transformation label from the EC classification of a particular enzyme.

## Oxidation and bond formation have specific meanings
Succinate dehydrogenase converts succinate to fumarate by removing reducing equivalents and introducing a carbon–carbon double bond. Four carbon atoms remain in the product: this is partial oxidation, not complete conversion of succinate carbon into CO2. The enzyme-associated flavin participates in electron transfer; in its respiratory context electrons pass onwards through the complex to quinone. Do not imagine a freely released soluble FADH2 molecule is the entire physiological electron-transfer pathway.

The pyruvate-carboxylase example joins a three-carbon skeleton with an inorganic carbon unit to form four-carbon oxaloacetate, using ATP-dependent activation and biotin chemistry. This can replenish a cycle intermediate. It is not evidence that every enzyme named “synthase” uses ATP in the same way. Classify the complete reaction rather than treating historical synthase/synthetase names as infallible rules.

## Overall rearrangement and enzyme mechanism can differ
Citrate and isocitrate interconvert through cis-aconitate. The hydroxyl position changes in the overall transformation, preparing a different arrangement for later reactions. However, aconitase, accepted as aconitate hydratase, is **EC 4.2.1.3**, a lyase/hydro-lyase. It performs dehydration followed by hydration. The lecturer's isomerisation heading describes the overall rearrangement; it must not be converted into an incorrect claim that aconitase is EC 5. The IUBMB entry explicitly lists both partial reactions.

This distinction is a useful application of Unit 8 classification. Reading only an overall category word can miss the enzyme's actual classified chemistry. The net reaction, elementary transformations and formal EC entry answer different questions.

## Phosphorylation, hydrolysis and carbon cleavage
Hexokinase transfers a phosphoryl group from ATP to glucose, producing glucose-6-phosphate and ADP in an appropriate ion/proton balance. The charged product is less able to cross the membrane by simple diffusion or the ordinary glucose transporter, helping retain and direct it within a cell. It is not necessarily permanently trapped: transporters, dephosphorylation and tissue context matter. Glucose-6-phosphate can enter several routes, so its formation is not automatically commitment exclusively to glycolysis.

Sucrose hydrolysis uses water to cleave a glycosidic linkage and yields glucose plus fructose. This differs from aldolase cleavage of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into the three-carbon products dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. The latter is lyase-type carbon–carbon cleavage, not an ordinary glycosidic hydrolysis merely because one molecule becomes two. Carbon accounting gives 6=3+3.

## A reaction can prepare the next step
Enolase removes water from 2-phosphoglycerate to form phosphoenolpyruvate. The new double-bond/enol-phosphate arrangement gives the following pyruvate-kinase reaction a high phosphoryl-transfer potential. PEP can transfer phosphate to ADP to form ATP; favourable product rearrangement contributes to the overall driving force. The energy balance belongs to the complete transformation rather than being “stored” in an isolated bond that releases energy simply because it is broken.

These are selected examples supporting pathway principles. They do not claim to be a complete glycolytic or citric-cycle syllabus, an exhaustive enzyme inventory or a universal ATP yield for glucose. All associated numerical questions state the atom count or assumptions they actually assess.

## Short concept checks
- Does succinate→fumarate remove all carbon as CO2? **Answer:** No. Both are four-carbon compounds; the step is a partial oxidation with a new double bond.
- Why is citrate→isocitrate not sufficient reason to label aconitase EC 5? **Answer:** Its classified dehydration/rehydration activity is EC 4.2.1.3, even though the overall transformation rearranges the molecule.

## Key takeaways
Inspect the transformed bond, carrier and atom balance. Overall labels and formal enzyme classes need to be reconciled explicitly.
