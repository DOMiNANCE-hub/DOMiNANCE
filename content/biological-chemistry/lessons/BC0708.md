# Secondary structure: helices, sheets, turns and collagen

## Learning goals
Explain how backbone hydrogen bonds organise local structure, compare α helices and β sheets and distinguish the collagen triple helix from an α helix.

## Local organisation is more than an overall shape
Secondary structure describes recurring local backbone conformations. It differs from primary sequence and from a chain's complete three-dimensional fold. Hydrogen bonds between backbone carbonyl oxygens and suitable N–H groups help stabilise regular patterns. Side chains influence which conformations are favourable, but the characteristic α-helix and β-sheet hydrogen bonds are not simply bonds between successive R groups. Water competes for these groups, so favourable geometry and the whole folded environment matter.

The familiar α helix of ordinary L-residue proteins is right-handed. Its backbone coils while side chains project outward. A regular hydrogen bond links the carbonyl oxygen of residue i to the backbone N–H of residue i+4. This is not a peptide bond across four residues; backbone covalent connectivity remains sequential. A typical ideal α helix has about 3.6 residues per turn and a rise of about 0.15 nm per residue, useful model values rather than a promise that every helix has identical geometry.

## Worked helix geometry and hydrogen-bond counting
For an ideal twenty-residue uninterrupted α-helical segment, the simple i→i+4 pattern permits 20−4=16 internal hydrogen bonds of that type. End groups and interactions outside the segment may differ. The axial length estimate is 20×0.15=3.0 nm using the supplied rise per residue. The result is an idealised length estimate, not a measured structure or the fully stretched peptide length.

Glycine's flexibility can impose an entropy cost for adopting a regular helix; proline restricts geometry and lacks an internal N–H donor. They often interrupt standard helices, but position and context matter. An α helix can contain both hydrophobic and hydrophilic faces, allowing an amphipathic surface helix or a membrane-spanning segment to serve different roles.

## β sheets connect neighbouring backbone strands
A β strand is more extended than an α helix. Several strands align side by side and hydrogen-bond through backbone groups to form a sheet, which is usually twisted rather than perfectly flat. R groups alternate towards opposite sides of a strand. Neighbouring strands can be parallel, running in the same N→C direction, or antiparallel, running in opposite directions. They can come from distant parts of one chain or from different chains; the defining pattern is not restricted to one of those possibilities.

A sheet hydrogen bond does not universally use the α-helical i+4 rule. Loops and turns connect secondary elements; a loop may be organised and functional even without a repeating helix/sheet pattern. A β turn reverses chain direction over a short segment, with glycine and proline often useful at particular positions. “Nonhelical” does not mean biologically useless or necessarily completely disordered.

## Collagen uses a different hierarchy
Collagen contains three chains wound into a triple-helical assembly. The repeating Gly–X–Y sequence puts small glycine at every third position, enabling tight packing; proline and hydroxyproline are frequent in the other positions. Each chain has an extended left-handed helical conformation, while the overall triple helix is a distinct superhelical arrangement. It is not three ordinary α helices. Subsequent extracellular assemblies and cross-links contribute to tensile strength.

Hydroxylation helps collagen maturation and depends on appropriate enzymatic conditions including vitamin C support. Substitution of a required glycine by a bulky residue can disrupt packing. This is a structural explanation, not a claim that every collagen variant produces an identical disease or that one diagram diagnoses a patient.

## Short concept checks
- In an α helix, does i→i+4 represent a new covalent cross-link? **Answer:** No. It is the characteristic backbone hydrogen-bond relationship; peptide bonds still join sequential residues.
- How do antiparallel β strands differ from parallel ones? **Answer:** Their N→C directions oppose rather than match one another.

## Key takeaways
Specify backbone geometry, hydrogen-bond pattern and direction. Collagen's triple helix and an ordinary α helix are different structures.
