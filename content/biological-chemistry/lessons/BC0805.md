# Measuring initial rates, enzyme activity and units

## Learning goals
Extract an initial rate from suitable data, distinguish concentration/time from amount/time and convert activity units while retaining assay conditions and blanks.

## Measure a rate before fitting a mechanism
Reaction rate describes change in concentration or amount per unit time with a stated stoichiometric convention. For S→P with coefficients one, product appearance and substrate disappearance have equal magnitude. If two product molecules form per reaction event, the product slope must be divided by two to express reaction-event rate. State what is being reported instead of hiding stoichiometry in a number.

An initial-rate assay measures an early approximately linear interval after mixing and any resolved transient. Little substrate has been depleted and little product accumulated. Later slowing can reflect substrate loss, reverse reaction, product inhibition or enzyme instability. A straight-line fit across an entire curved progress trace would conceal those differences.

Control temperature, pH, ionic conditions, enzyme amount and relevant cosubstrate concentrations. Use reagent blanks and appropriate controls for background signal, substrate autoconversion or interfering absorbance. A coloured signal may require a supplied calibration or extinction coefficient; detector slope is not automatically molar reaction rate.

## Worked initial-rate and volume conversion
Suppose a hypothetical 2.0 mL reaction gives blank-corrected product concentrations 0, 3 and 6 µmol/L at 0, 10 and 20 seconds, followed by 7 µmol/L at 40 seconds. The initial linear rate is 6/20=0.30 µmol L−1 s−1, or 0.30 µM/s. Using all forty seconds would give 7/40=0.175 µM/s and underestimate that early slope.

The initial amount rate is 0.30 µmol L−1 s−1 ×0.002 L=0.00060 µmol/s. Multiply by 60 to obtain 0.036 µmol/min. An enzyme activity unit U, often called IU in the source, represents 1 µmol transformed per minute under specified conditions; this sample therefore has 0.036 U in the stated assay. Keeping concentration rate and total activity separate prevents a thousandfold volume-unit mistake.

## Katal and specific activity
One katal is 1 mol/s. Converting to µmol/min gives 10^6×60=6×10^7 U. Conversely, 1 U=(10−6/60) mol/s≈1.667×10−8 kat=16.67 nkat. The conversion concerns defined activity, not a universal number of enzyme molecules. Different conditions can give different activities for the same physical sample.

Specific activity is activity divided by total protein mass, such as U/mg, and is useful in purification. It differs from the turnover constant in s−1, which requires molar catalytic-centre information. A fivefold enrichment in U/mg does not necessarily mean fivefold product recovery or fivefold intrinsic improvement of every enzyme molecule; contaminants may simply have been removed.

## Choosing comparisons responsibly
Doubling active enzyme concentration doubles the initial rate only within a suitable range where substrate supply, detector response and other components are not limiting. Apparent activity can decrease through dilution of required partners or adsorption as well as genuine catalytic changes. For a coupled assay, the reporting reaction must be sufficiently fast not to limit the target reaction.

These numerical traces are constructed teaching examples, not measurements made in this project. Replicates, uncertainties, calibration and a justified fit interval would be required before interpreting a real experiment or using an activity result clinically.

## Short concept checks
- Is 0.30 µM/s already a total activity in µmol/min? **Answer:** No. Multiply by reaction volume in litres and convert seconds to minutes.
- Does one activity unit specify a fixed enzyme molecule count? **Answer:** No. Activity depends on catalytic behaviour and assay conditions; a molar amount requires additional information.

## Key takeaways
Specify slope interval, stoichiometry, volume and conditions. U, kat, U/mg and s−1 describe different quantities.
