# Polysaccharide architecture: starch, glycogen and branching

## Learning goals
Classify glycans by composition and topology. Explain the structures of amylose, amylopectin and glycogen and connect branching to mobilisation without confusing reducing and nonreducing ends.

## Composition and topology are independent
A homopolysaccharide uses one principal monosaccharide type; a heteropolysaccharide uses more than one. A glucan uses glucose residues, while a mannan uses mannose residues. Galacturonan uses galacturonic-acid residues; the source's label galactose omits the uronic-acid distinction. Either composition class can have additional variation in branching, linkages and substitutions.

Amylose is predominantly an unbranched α(1→4)-glucan. Amylopectin has α(1→4) chains with α(1→6) branch points. Starch contains both, with proportions that depend on plant source and preparation. The lecture's percentage ranges illustrate typical compositions rather than universal limits for every starch.

Glycogen also has α(1→4) chains and α(1→6) branches, generally more frequent than amylopectin's in the introductory comparison. It is an important animal glucose store in liver and muscle. The source's branch intervals and tissue mass percentages are approximate contextual figures, not exact counts for every molecule or physiological state.

## Why branches support rapid mobilisation
Most chain-extending and chain-shortening activities considered here act at nonreducing ends. More branches provide more such terminal sites for concurrent enzyme action. The branch linkage itself needs the appropriate handling; an α(1→4)-specific enzyme does not automatically cut an α(1→6) point. Branching can therefore increase accessible ends while creating chemically distinct junctions.

A connected, ordinary branched glucan tree has one potential reducing-root region and many nonreducing ends. Each added branch normally adds a nonreducing terminus, not a new free anomeric carbon. In biological glycogen the root is associated with glycogenin, so the chemical accessibility of that reducing region needs qualification. Long polymers have very low reducing-end concentration relative to residue concentration, and may not show a conspicuous small-sugar copper reaction. A possible terminal group and a strong positive bulk test are not equivalent claims.

## Worked branch counting with stated assumptions
Begin with one acyclic linear chain: one nonreducing end and one reducing-root region. Add two side chains through ordinary glycosidic branch connections without making cycles. There are now three nonreducing ends, while the root count remains one. If the whole connected structure contains 20 residues, it still has 19 linking bonds. This example depends on the tree assumption; covalent cycles and cross-links require a new count.

## Short concept checks
- Does an α(1→6) branch create a second free reducing centre? **Answer:** Not in the ordinary connected glucan tree; the added chain's anomeric carbon forms its connecting bond, while a new nonreducing terminus appears.
- Which starch component has branches at α(1→6) points? **Answer:** Amylopectin; amylose is predominantly α(1→4) and unbranched in this comparison.

## Key takeaways
Composition, linkage and topology all matter. Branching changes enzyme access and terminal-site number without making every branch a reducing end.
