# Reaction-based enzyme nomenclature and seven EC classes

## Learning goals
Classify a reaction by what is transformed, interpret an EC identifier and distinguish transfer, hydrolysis, nonhydrolytic cleavage, joining and coupled translocation.

## Classify the reaction rather than the name alone
Traditional names may end in –ase, as in urease, but trypsin and pepsin show that the suffix is not compulsory. An EC number identifies a classified enzymatic activity by reaction. It is not a gene accession, amino-acid sequence, organism name or indication of how many subunits a protein has. Distinct proteins can catalyse the same classified reaction, and one multifunctional protein can have more than one activity.

The available Lecture 8 prose and table already include seven classes. This was independently checked against the NC-IUBMB nomenclature resource: translocases form EC 7. Older six-class accounts are historical, not the classification taught here. Individual entries can be transferred or revised, so exact numbers should be verified rather than guessed from an enzyme's common name.

## Seven useful reaction questions
| Class | Main chemical question | Illustrative activity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EC 1 Oxidoreductases | Is there an oxidation–reduction transformation? | Dehydrogenase transferring reducing equivalents |
| EC 2 Transferases | Is a group transferred from one molecule to another? | Kinase transferring a phosphoryl group |
| EC 3 Hydrolases | Is a bond cleaved using water? | Protease hydrolysing a peptide linkage |
| EC 4 Lyases | Is there nonhydrolytic cleavage/addition, often involving a double bond? | Fumarase hydrating a double bond in its reversible reaction |
| EC 5 Isomerases | Is the arrangement changed within one molecular formula? | Glucose-phosphate isomerisation |
| EC 6 Ligases | Are molecules joined with coupling to nucleoside-triphosphate breakdown? | Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase activity |
| EC 7 Translocases | Is a coupled reaction linked to movement across or separation within a membrane? | An appropriately classified ATP-driven ion-translocating activity |

A hydratase can add water across a double bond without being a hydrolase cleaving a substrate bond by water. A kinase transfers phosphate from a donor; a phosphatase hydrolyses an appropriate phosphate linkage. Recognise reactants and products rather than associating any water or phosphate with a single class.

## Reading the four fields
In an identifier EC a.b.c.d, the first field specifies the major class, subsequent fields refine the reaction grouping and the final field distinguishes the entry within that group. For the lecturer's EC 3.4.21.4 example, first field 3 places the activity among hydrolases. It does not mean three substrates or four subunits. Subclass meanings depend on the class, so no universal substrate-count rule applies to the other digits.

## Coupled transport needs the full activity
EC 7 groups include translocation linked to redox reactions, nucleoside-triphosphate hydrolysis and other specified processes. A protein that oxidises a substrate and couples that reaction to ion movement may therefore need the complete coupled reaction considered. Merely reducing a quinone does not prove every such enzyme belongs to EC 7. Likewise, passive passage through any channel is not automatically an EC 7 catalytic activity.

## Worked decision sequence
For reaction R–O–PO3 + H2O → R–OH + phosphate, identify cleavage using water: a hydrolase-type activity is indicated. For ATP + acceptor–OH → ADP + acceptor–O–phosphate, identify group transfer: a transferase-type kinase is indicated. For two molecules joined using an explicitly coupled ATP-consuming reaction, consider a ligase, while verifying the full reaction before assigning a precise entry.

## Short concept checks
- Does an EC number uniquely identify one gene sequence? **Answer:** No. It classifies catalytic activity; different proteins can share an activity.
- Why is a kinase not classified as a hydrolase merely because ATP participates? **Answer:** Its classified activity transfers a phosphoryl group to an acceptor rather than simply hydrolysing that group with water.

## Key takeaways
Read the complete reaction. Seven classes are supported by both the supplied lecture and the current nomenclature resource; exact entry numbers require specific evidence.
