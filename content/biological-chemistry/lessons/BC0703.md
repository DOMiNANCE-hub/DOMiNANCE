# Amino-acid ionisation, buffering and isoelectric calculations

## Learning goals
Track charge as pH changes, use supplied pKa values for simple calculations, distinguish pI from pKa and choose the two dissociations that bracket a net-zero state.

## Proton transfer changes charge without making a peptide
A carboxyl group can lose H+ to give COO−; an ammonium group can lose H+ to give neutral amine. Lower pH favours protonated states; higher pH favours deprotonated states. A zwitterion contains opposite formal charges even when their sum is zero. Zero net charge does not mean no charges, no polarity or complete insolubility.

For one acid–base pair, pH = pKa + log10([deprotonated]/[protonated]). At pH=pKa the two forms have equal concentrations. One pH unit above pKa gives a 10:1 ratio favouring the deprotonated form; one below gives 1:10. A buffering region near pKa arises because appreciable amounts of both forms can accept added acid or base. A molecule with several groups has several dissociations, not one universal pKa.

## Worked two-group example
For an illustrative glycine-like free amino acid, use supplied pKa values 2.4 for COOH and 9.6 for NH3+. At very low pH, NH3+ and COOH give net +1. After the first dissociation, NH3+ and COO− give net 0. After the second, NH2 and COO− give net −1. Around pH 7 the zwitterionic form predominates; the calculation describes an equilibrium population, not an absolute state of every molecule.

The isoelectric point pI is the pH at which the population's average net charge is zero. In this ideal two-group example, the neutral state lies between pKa 2.4 and 9.6, so pI=(2.4+9.6)/2=6.0. Averaging arbitrary pKa values would give the wrong answer for a side-chain-ionising amino acid.

At pH 8.6, for the ammonium dissociation alone, [NH2]/[NH3+]=10^(8.6−9.6)=0.1. The ammonium-protonated fraction is 1/(1+0.1)=0.909, about 91%. The carboxyl is already overwhelmingly deprotonated under this simplified model, so the mean charge is approximately +0.909−1=−0.091, not exactly zero. Supply the model and numbers rather than treating this as a measured pKa for every protein-bound glycine.

## Worked side-chain examples
For a lysine-like free amino acid with supplied successive pKa values 2.2, 9.0 and 10.5, track charges +2→+1→0→−1 as pH rises. The zero state is bracketed by 9.0 and 10.5, giving pI=9.75. Averaging 2.2 and 9.0 would bracket the wrong state.

For an aspartate-like example with pKa 2.0, 3.9 and 9.9, charges go +1→0→−1→−2. Now the pI uses the first two: (2.0+3.9)/2=2.95. A quick acidic-versus-basic expectation helps catch errors, but following the charges is the reliable method.

With a supplied histidine side-chain pKa 6.0 at pH 7.0, deprotonated/protonated=10. The positively charged fraction is 1/11≈9.1%. This explains why classifying histidine as basic does not mean every histidine side chain is fully positive at pH 7.

## Applying pI to proteins
Protein pI depends on terminal groups, all ionisable side chains and their environment. It is not the arithmetic mean of the free amino-acid pIs. Below its pI a protein tends to have positive net charge; above, negative. This can guide electrophoresis and ion-exchange choices. Solubility often decreases near pI because repulsion is reduced, but precipitation is not guaranteed for every protein or buffer.

## Short concept checks
- Does pH=pI mean every group is uncharged? **Answer:** No. Average positive and negative contributions cancel; charged groups can remain present.
- Which lysine-like values bracket pI in the supplied +2→+1→0→−1 sequence? **Answer:** 9.0 and 10.5, the dissociations immediately before and after the zero-charge state.

## Key takeaways
State the supplied pKa values and assumptions. Track each charge transition before selecting a pI formula; use equilibrium ratios rather than all-or-none protonation.
