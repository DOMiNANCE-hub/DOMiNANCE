# Functional groups and biochemical behaviour

## Learning goals
Recognise common functional groups in condensed structures. Connect electron distribution, hydrogen bonding, acid–base behaviour and bond context to biological properties without assuming one group determines an entire molecule's behaviour.

## Recognise the local arrangement
A functional group is a characteristic arrangement of atoms and bonds that contributes to recurring chemical behaviour. R usually denotes a variable organic group; it is not an element symbol. The same molecule can contain several functional groups whose effects interact. Identify connectivity before assigning the group.

| Group | Common representation | Useful distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | R–OH | Has an O–H donor; not a carboxylic acid |
| Ether | R–O–R′ | Oxygen may accept H bonds; no O–H donor |
| Aldehyde | R–C(=O)–H | Carbonyl carbon has an attached H |
| Ketone | R–C(=O)–R′ | Carbonyl between carbon groups |
| Carboxylic acid | R–C(=O)–OH | Can deprotonate to carboxylate |
| Ester | R–C(=O)–O–R′ | Carbonyl plus O linked to another carbon group |
| Amide | R–C(=O)–N… | Nitrogen directly attached to carbonyl carbon |
| Amine | R–NH₂, R₂NH or R₃N | Basicity depends on structure and environment |
| Thiol / disulfide | R–SH / R–S–S–R′ | Distinct sulfur arrangements and reactivity |
| Phosphate-containing group | P bonded through O in an appropriate structure | Charge and acid–base state require context |

## Worked amine versus amide
Compare CH₃CH₂NH₂ with CH₃C(=O)NH₂. Both contain nitrogen, but only the second is an amide because nitrogen directly attaches to a carbonyl carbon. Its lone pair participates in resonance with that carbonyl, changing basicity and restricting C–N rotation relative to a simple amine. The peptide linkage is an amide, so its behaviour cannot be inferred by treating it as an ordinary freely rotating amine bond.

Compare CH₃C(=O)OH with CH₃C(=O)OCH₃. The first is a carboxylic acid; the second is an ester. Replacing the acidic O–H with O–CH₃ changes acid–base behaviour and reaction possibilities. Both contain carbonyl oxygen, so “contains oxygen” is not a sufficiently specific classification.

## Charge, water and combined effects
The charge of an ionisable group depends on pH, its acid–base equilibrium and its molecular environment. A carboxyl group can exist as neutral COOH or anionic COO−; an amine can accept a proton under suitable conditions. “All amines are positively charged” or “all acids are always completely ionised” is too absolute. Later units develop the quantitative equilibrium framework.

Adding a hydroxyl can increase favourable interactions with water, but one hydroxyl does not make every large hydrocarbon fully miscible. Chain length, additional groups, charge and the overall free-energy balance matter. Functional-group recognition gives a starting hypothesis, not a complete solubility calculation.

## Connections to later units
Carbohydrates contain carbonyl-derived and hydroxyl chemistry, including ring formation that changes the visible free carbonyl. Nucleotides combine a base, sugar and phosphate-containing structures. Proteins contain peptide amides and varied side-chain groups. Lipids include esters or amides in some classes, while other lipids lack those linkages. Do not define all lipids as esters or assume every biomolecule contains each group.

The source's functional-group figure also includes arenes, haloalkanes, acid anhydrides and acyl halides. Recognise arene as an aromatic framework, haloalkane as a carbon–halogen arrangement, acid anhydride as two acyl groups linked through oxygen, and acyl halide as an acyl carbon attached to a halogen. These are distinct structural categories; their detailed reaction chemistry is not established by a single icon.

## Short concept checks
- Which feature distinguishes an amide from an ordinary amine? **Answer:** The amide nitrogen is directly bonded to a carbonyl carbon.
- Is every molecule with a carbonyl group a ketone? **Answer:** No. Aldehydes, acids, esters, amides and other groups also contain carbonyls with different attached atoms.

## Key takeaways
Read the bond network, identify every relevant group and then consider charge and environment. Functional-group patterns connect this unit to later biological chemistry without replacing detailed molecular reasoning.
