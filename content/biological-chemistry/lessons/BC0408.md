# Conformations and rotational energy barriers

## Learning goals
Distinguish conformation from configuration. Use a viewing direction to compare staggered, eclipsed and anti/gauche arrangements without implying that rotation costs no energy.

## Same connectivity, different spatial arrangement
Conformers interconvert through rotations about suitable bonds without changing connectivity or necessarily breaking a sigma bond. A chain drawn as a zigzag is a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional arrangement; its shape can change as bonds rotate. The arrangements have different energies and populations rather than being automatically equally likely.

A Newman projection looks along a selected bond. The front atom and its substituents are viewed against the rear atom and its substituents. State the viewing bond and direction before comparing two projections. In an eclipsed arrangement, front and rear bonds align in the view; in a staggered arrangement they are offset. The drawing is a projection, not evidence that substituents occupy the same physical position.

Ethane has a torsional barrier, with eclipsed arrangements higher in energy than staggered ones in the simple comparison. “Free rotation about single bonds” means rotation is much easier than breaking the sigma bond or disrupting an ordinary isolated double bond, not that the rotational energy surface is flat. Bonding, repulsion and electron interactions determine the barrier.

## Worked butane comparison
Look along butane's central C–C bond. Each of those two carbons bears one methyl group and two hydrogens. In the anti staggered conformer, the two methyl groups are approximately 180° apart in the projection. In a gauche staggered conformer, they are approximately 60° apart. Both are staggered, but their energies differ because the larger groups interact differently. “Staggered” alone does not identify the lowest-energy member of every molecule.

An eclipsed arrangement with the methyl groups aligned is particularly unfavourable in the introductory butane comparison. Rotating the bond changes the dihedral angle continuously; it does not turn butane into 2-methylpropane or change a stereocentre's configuration by simply relabelling the page.

|---|---|---|
| Constitutional isomers | Different | Requires chemical change of connectivity |
| Conformers | Same | Rotation about suitable bonds |
| Alkene E/Z configurations | Same | Restricted by the pi interaction; not ordinary free rotation |

## Constraints in biological molecules
Rings constrain rotations, and even a single bond can have restricted rotation when conjugation changes its electronic character. The peptide C–N bond has partial double-bond character from resonance, helping constrain protein-backbone geometry; it is not a freely rotating alkane-like bond. Other backbone bonds permit conformational variation subject to steric and energetic limits.

This connects molecular structure to protein folding without teaching that every observed fold is the conformation of one isolated molecule in a vacuum. Solvent, temperature, intramolecular interactions and partners influence biological conformational populations. A flexible molecule can have a preferred conformation, and a rigid region can still undergo limited motion.

## Short concept checks
- Are anti and gauche butane different constitutional isomers? **Answer:** No. They have the same connectivity and differ by rotation about the central bond.
- Does a single bond guarantee completely unrestricted rotation? **Answer:** No. Torsional barriers, conjugation and rings can constrain it.

## Key takeaways
State the projection and bond being viewed. Rotations change conformation on an energy surface; connectivity and configurational assignments require separate analysis.
