# Oxidised and reduced monosaccharide derivatives

## Learning goals
Distinguish sugar acids by the carbon oxidised, explain carbonyl reduction to alditols and recognise the chemistry behind representative biological applications.

## Oxidation depends on position
Glucose has an aldehyde at C1 in its open-chain form and a primary alcohol at C6. Oxidising C1 gives gluconic acid, an aldonic acid. Oxidising C6 while retaining the anomeric system gives glucuronic acid, a uronic acid. Oxidising both ends gives glucaric acid, an aldaric acid. The similar names describe different functional changes and must not be used interchangeably. At physiological pH many carboxyl groups are predominantly carboxylate, so names ending in -ate can refer to the corresponding ion.

| Transformation of glucose | Product family | Example |
|---|---|---|
| C1 aldehyde → carboxyl | Aldonic acid | Gluconic acid/gluconate |
| C6 primary alcohol → carboxyl | Uronic acid | Glucuronic acid/glucuronate |
| Both termini oxidised | Aldaric acid | Glucaric acid/glucarate |

Uronic acids contribute charge to extracellular glycans. Activated glucuronic-acid donors can also be used in conjugation reactions that alter the handling of other compounds. That is a linked biochemical application, not a claim that free glucuronic acid spontaneously detoxifies everything. Galacturonic acid is derived from galactose's terminal oxidation; galacturonans are polymers of galacturonic-acid residues, not merely galactose despite the slide's abbreviated label.

## Lactones are cyclic esters
A carboxyl group and an OH within a sugar acid can form a cyclic ester called a lactone. This is chemically different from a hemiacetal, whose anomeric carbon bears OH and OR. The source shows gluconic-acid/lactone interconversion; its drawing should be read as a functional-group comparison, not a complete balanced acid–base equation under every condition. Identify carbonyl location and ester linkage before naming the ring.

## Carbonyl reduction gives sugar alcohols
Reducing a sugar carbonyl to an alcohol gives an alditol. Glucose reduction produces glucitol, commonly called sorbitol. Ribose can give ribitol. A ketose's carbonyl carbon may become a new stereocentre on reduction, so fructose can produce both sorbitol and mannitol rather than one obligatory product. The source also depicts myo-inositol among sugar-alcohol examples; it is a cyclitol, a cyclic polyol, rather than a direct linear glucose-carbonyl reduction product. Oxidation and reduction change functional groups; they are not the same as α/β anomerisation.

A clinical connection is accumulation of sugar alcohols in particular metabolic settings, such as galactitol in disorders of galactose handling. Such an example identifies a pathway consequence, not a diagnostic test or a guarantee that every sugar alcohol is harmful. Product, tissue, enzyme activity and concentration all matter.

## Short concept checks
- Which change converts glucose to glucuronic rather than gluconic acid? **Answer:** Oxidation of the terminal C6 primary alcohol, rather than the C1 aldehyde.
- Does reduction of a carbonyl necessarily create only one stereochemical product? **Answer:** No. Reduction of an appropriate ketose can create a new stereocentre and yield different alditols.

## Key takeaways
Locate the changed carbon before interpreting a derivative name. Aldonic, uronic, aldaric, lactone and alditol describe distinct chemical features.
