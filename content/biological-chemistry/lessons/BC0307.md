# Atomic and ionic size: attraction, shielding and comparison

## Learning goals
Explain broad radius trends using shell extent and effective nuclear attraction. Compare ions correctly, including isoelectronic series, and distinguish different operational radius definitions.

## Why radius is a defined measurement
An electron cloud has no sharp outer edge, so atomic radius requires a convention. A covalent radius can be obtained from half the internuclear distance in an appropriate homonuclear bond. Metallic and nonbonded van der Waals radii describe different environments. Mixing definitions can create an apparent trend reversal that should not be explained by an invented universal repulsion rule.

Across many main-group periods, added protons increase nuclear attraction while added electrons enter the same principal shell. Shielding does not fully cancel the increased nuclear charge, so covalent radii generally decrease. Down a group, occupation of higher shells and greater shielding generally produce larger atoms. Effective nuclear charge is the net nuclear attraction experienced by an electron after shielding effects; it is not the charge of one proton or identical for every electron in an atom.

A cation is generally smaller than its parent neutral atom. Removing outer electrons may remove an occupied shell, and the remaining electrons experience a changed balance of repulsion and attraction. An anion is generally larger than its parent atom because adding electrons increases repulsion and changes the electron distribution while nuclear charge remains fixed. Neither observation means the nucleus gains or loses protons during ionisation.

For an isoelectronic series, compare species with the same electron count. More protons generally attract that same electron population more strongly, giving a smaller radius. This controlled comparison differs from blindly reading a single arrow across unlike charged ions.

## Worked radius and ranking
A homonuclear molecule has an internuclear distance of 244 pm in the stated bonding convention. Each atom's covalent radius is 244/2 = 122 pm, or 0.122 nm. The calculation does not automatically give its van der Waals radius or the radius of an ion in a crystal.

O²−, F−, Ne, Na+ and Mg²+ each have ten electrons. Their proton numbers are 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12. Within this simplified isoelectronic comparison, increasing attraction gives decreasing size in that order. Comparing measured neutral-neon and ionic radii still requires compatible definitions; an examination ranking model should specify the intended comparison.

Within the halides, F−, Cl− and Br− generally grow larger down the group as occupied shells extend outward. The lecturer's text reverses the down-group anion trend; that reversal is not adopted. The “increase from halogen to noble gas” note also requires consistent radius definitions rather than a universal claim about electron repulsion.

## Biological relevance and traps
Ion size and hydration influence how ions interact with water and protein sites. A bare-ion radius ranking alone does not establish the size of a hydrated complex or prove which ion crosses a particular channel. Channel selectivity requires the specific protein environment and energetic trade-offs, not merely “the smaller ion always passes”.

## Short concept checks
- Which is generally smaller in the isoelectronic pair Na+ and Mg²+, and why? **Answer:** Mg²+, because twelve protons attract the same ten electrons more strongly than eleven protons.
- Does gaining one electron increase the nuclear charge of fluorine? **Answer:** No. F− still has nine protons; its electron population and distribution change.

## Key takeaways
Specify the radius convention and comparison set. Broad trends arise from attraction, shielding and shell structure; same-electron-count comparisons are particularly useful when interpreted within their assumptions.
