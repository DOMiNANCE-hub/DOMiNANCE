# Covalent modification, enzyme abundance and protein modulators

## Learning goals
Compare reversible phosphorylation with changes in enzyme amount, explain a regulatory protein complex and distinguish kinase and phosphatase chemistry.

## Phosphorylation changes selected molecular properties
A protein kinase transfers a phosphoryl group from an appropriate donor, commonly ATP, to a suitable protein group. Many regulatory examples involve Ser, Thr or Tyr hydroxyls. A phosphatase hydrolyses the phosphate linkage, releasing inorganic phosphate. Dephosphorylation is not simply the kinase reaction run backwards to regenerate ATP from ADP; the chemical reactants and energy balance differ.

Adding phosphate changes charge, hydrogen-bonding possibilities and recognition by other proteins. It can shift a conformation or create a docking site. Depending on the target and site, it may increase activity, decrease it, alter localisation or change partner binding. The source's active-to-inactive example is one possible outcome, not a universal phosphorylation switch direction.

Reversible covalent modification differs from irreversible proteolytic activation, discussed next, and from noncovalent effector binding. Several mechanisms can operate together on the same protein. A measured phosphate level by itself does not establish the activity of every enzyme in a cell.

## Protein partners can restrain or enable catalysis
In the familiar model of cAMP-dependent protein kinase A, an inactive holoenzyme contains regulatory and catalytic subunits, commonly described as R2C2. cAMP binding to regulatory subunits favours release or functional liberation of catalytic activity in the classical model. The regulatory partner acts through association rather than being consumed as substrate for each target phosphorylation. Cellular complexes can constrain the precise physical behaviour; the schematic is not a claim that every protein kinase works this way.

The lecturer also describes a phosphorylated inhibitor protein that binds and inhibits a protein phosphatase. This illustrates layered control: phosphorylation of one protein can change another protein's activity. It does not imply that every phosphorylated inhibitor has the same target or that every phosphatase is inhibited by every phosphorylated protein.

## Changing enzyme amount usually involves another timescale
Gene expression controls synthesis; regulated degradation controls lifetime. Increasing enzyme abundance can increase pathway capacity, but changes develop according to synthesis, maturation, turnover and availability of partners. The source's minutes-versus-hours comparison illustrates relative timescales rather than a universal boundary separating bacteria and eukaryotes. Rapid degradation or pre-existing transcripts can complicate the comparison.

If the active enzyme amount doubles and the simple assay remains otherwise identical, Vmax doubles and intrinsic Km need not change. However, abundance does not guarantee doubled cellular flux when substrate supply or another step limits the pathway. Always distinguish enzyme concentration, active fraction and the properties of each functioning centre.

## Worked two-factor comparison
Suppose a preparation has 10 nM total equivalent enzyme centres, but only 20% are active. Its active concentration is 2 nM. A signal that increases the active fraction to 80% without changing total abundance gives 8 nM active centres: a fourfold increase in model Vmax. Alternatively doubling the total amount at the original 20% fraction gives 4 nM active centres: a twofold increase. The same total protein assay cannot distinguish these regulatory interventions without information about active fraction.

These calculations hold kcat and assay conditions fixed. If a modification also changes per-centre catalysis, the effect must be analysed separately. This is why a whole-cell activity change should not automatically be attributed only to transcription or only to phosphorylation.

## Short concept checks
- Does a phosphatase ordinarily reverse kinase chemistry by making ATP from ADP? **Answer:** No. It hydrolyses the phosphate linkage, usually releasing inorganic phosphate.
- Must phosphorylation always inhibit the modified enzyme? **Answer:** No. The result depends on the specific protein, site and regulatory context.

## Key takeaways
Track amount, active fraction, per-centre behaviour and partner association separately. Reversible modification is one coordinated control, not a universal on/off code.
