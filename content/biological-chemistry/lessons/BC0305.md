# Transition atoms, ions and configuration exceptions

## Learning goals
Write the lecturer's chromium and copper exceptions accurately. Construct common ionic configurations and explain why “all cations have a noble-gas configuration” is false.

## Close energies require care
The 3d and 4s subshells have similar energies whose relationship changes with electron occupancy and ionisation. A simple filling-order mnemonic cannot capture every many-electron energy contribution. Chromium's neutral ground configuration is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and copper's is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, as recorded in reference data. They differ from the naive predictions [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² and [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s².

Half-filled and filled subshells provide useful introductory patterns, but “every half-filled subshell always wins” is not a universal rule for inventing configurations. The actual ground state reflects the total many-electron energy, including electron interactions. Use established configurations for exceptions rather than applying an unlimited promotion rule.

When forming common first-row transition-metal cations, remove the outer 4s electrons before 3d electrons. This is not a contradiction that can be resolved by always reversing the neutral-atom mnemonic: the ion has a different electron distribution and energy ordering. Main-group ions such as Na+ or Cl− often attain a noble-gas configuration. Transition-metal cations commonly retain partially filled d subshells.

## Worked iron and copper ions
Neutral iron, Z = 26, is [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². Fe²+ has 24 electrons: remove both 4s electrons to obtain [Ar] 3d⁶. Fe³+ has 23 electrons: remove one additional 3d electron, giving [Ar] 3d⁵. Neither configuration is that of a noble gas. Check the totals: argon contributes 18; 18 + 6 = 24 and 18 + 5 = 23.

Neutral copper has 29 electrons and [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. Cu+ loses 4s¹ and becomes [Ar] 3d¹⁰; Cu²+ has [Ar] 3d⁹. A filled d subshell is not the same as a noble-gas configuration. Conversely, sodium loses its single 3s electron and becomes [Ne]. The examples show why the lecturer's blanket cation statement needs qualification.

The labels Fe²+ and Fe³+ describe charge for isolated ions. Oxidation-state notation in a coordinated biological complex is a useful formal electron-counting convention, but does not mean a metal in a protein behaves exactly like a free gaseous ion. Bonding to ligands and the surrounding structure matter. This unit introduces configuration; later bonding teaching develops that context.

## Common traps
Removing a 3d electron first from neutral Fe while keeping 4s² gives the wrong configuration for its common cations. Confusing atomic number with electron number makes an ion seem to change element: Fe³+ still has 26 protons. Writing [Ar] 3d⁵ for neutral chromium omits the remaining 4s electron and gives the wrong total.

## Short concept checks
- What is the configuration of Fe²+ from [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²? **Answer:** [Ar] 3d⁶; the two 4s electrons are removed first.
- Does the filled 3d¹⁰ configuration of Cu+ make it a noble gas? **Answer:** No. Its proton number and configuration are not those of a noble-gas atom; filled d and noble-gas configuration are different claims.

## Key takeaways
Use verified neutral-atom exceptions, then count electrons explicitly when forming ions. Main-group patterns do not justify a universal noble-gas rule for cations, especially transition metals.
