# Base composition, complementarity and Chargaff calculations

## Learning goals
Apply canonical duplex composition constraints, calculate base-pair and hydrogen-bond counts, and identify cases in which the simplified equalities do not apply.

## Why the equalities require a specified molecule
In an ordinary fully paired DNA duplex, each A pairs with T and each G with C. Across the complete duplex this gives A=T, G=C and total purines equal total pyrimidines. A purine–pyrimidine combination helps maintain consistent duplex width, but size alone is insufficient: donor/acceptor complementarity distinguishes canonical pairing from a mismatch such as A–C. A single strand need not satisfy A=T or G=C on its own. A partly paired molecule, unusual base chemistry or an unqualified mixture requires its actual structure rather than blind use of those equations.

RNA is often single-stranded with local folded regions, so whole-molecule A=U and G=C cannot be assumed for every cellular RNA. A fully complementary canonical double-stranded RNA can satisfy the analogous A=U and G=C relationships. Modified-base counting also needs an explicit convention; a derivatised cytosine should not simply vanish from a composition denominator without explanation.

## Worked percentage and count examples
If adenine is 30% of all bases in a canonical DNA duplex, thymine is also 30%. The remaining 40% is G+C, split equally into 20% each. GC content means G+C combined, not G=C interpreted as a product or a single 20% value. These fractions concern both strands together.

For a 1,000-base-pair duplex with 40% GC pairs, 400 pairs are G–C and 600 are A–T. There are 2,000 total nucleotide residues: 400 G,400 C,600 A,600 T. In the ideal canonical drawing, G–C has three interbase hydrogen bonds and A–T two, giving 400×3+600×2=2,400. The hydrogen-bond count is not the number of phosphodiester links or a complete prediction of duplex free energy.

## Composition is not sequence or a universal stability formula
Two DNA fragments can have identical base percentages but different sequences. Order determines coding and many recognition properties; composition alone cannot reconstruct the sequence. The lecturer's organism/tissue generalisations describe broad genomic comparisons, not a guarantee that every DNA compartment or every fragment has identical composition. Nuclear and organellar genomes, mutations and sampling can differ.

GC-rich duplexes often have higher melting temperatures than AT-rich comparators under otherwise similar conditions. The trend includes sequence-dependent base stacking and solvent/ion effects, not merely counting one extra hydrogen bond per GC pair. Length, salt, mismatch and concentration can also matter. An empirical trend should not become a universal linear law that ignores all other variables.

## Short concept checks
- If a complete canonical duplex is 20% G, what is its total GC content? **Answer:** 40%, because C also contributes 20%.
- Does A=T necessarily hold in an isolated single DNA strand? **Answer:** No. It follows across the complete canonically paired duplex, not every individual strand.

## Key takeaways
State the denominator, strand set and pairing assumptions before calculating. Composition constraints are powerful within their scope and misleading outside it.
