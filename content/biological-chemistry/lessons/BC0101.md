# Elements, atoms and biological organisation

## Learning goals
Distinguish an element from an atom, ion and isotope. Use proton number to identify an element, and explain why knowing the elements in a cell does not yet explain how that cell works.

## Element identity and organisation
An element is defined by the number of protons in its nuclei, its atomic number Z. Every carbon atom has six protons. Adding or removing a neutron produces a different isotope of carbon; adding or removing an electron produces an ion. Neither change turns carbon into a different element. Changing proton number requires a nuclear process and changes element identity.

Mass number A counts protons plus neutrons in one nucleus. It is an integer for a specified isotope. The relative atomic mass on a periodic table is usually an abundance-weighted value for an element's isotopic composition; it need not equal the mass number of any individual atom. Chemical reactions rearrange electrons and bonds, while nuclear reactions can rearrange nuclei. Burning carbon does not create oxygen nuclei.

Atoms form molecules or extended ionic structures. A molecule is a discrete bonded group of atoms: water contains hydrogen and oxygen in a defined arrangement. A solution may also contain separated hydrated ions such as Na+ and Cl−; calling every dissolved substance a molecule obscures this distinction. Molecules and ions participate in macromolecules, membranes and cellular structures. Cells organise these components into a functioning system, with regulated transport, energy transformations and information storage.

A list of ingredients cannot specify their arrangement. The same elements can occur in a living cell, its damaged remains and a bottle of extracted compounds. Organisation, chemical form and continuing processes explain differences that elemental composition alone cannot.

| Change | What changes? | Element identity |
|---|---|---|
| Gain or lose an electron | Charge | Retained |
| Change neutron number | Isotope | Retained |
| Change proton number | Atomic number | Changed |

## Worked example
An atom has six protons, seven neutrons and six electrons. First identify Z = 6: it is carbon. Next calculate A = 6 + 7 = 13: the isotope is carbon-13. Equal proton and electron numbers give zero net charge. If it loses one electron, five electrons remain and the net charge is +1; it is still carbon-13. If instead its nucleus gained a proton, it would cease to be carbon.

Now compare a water molecule with a sodium ion. Water contains atoms of two elements bonded together and is electrically neutral overall. Na+ is one atom with fewer electrons than protons. Both can be cellular constituents, but they are different chemical entities.

## Misconceptions and limits
“An isotope is a different element” confuses neutron number with proton number. “All atoms of an element have the same mass” overlooks isotopes. “Cells are just collections of elements” overlooks molecular structure and interactions. These distinctions matter later when interpreting radioactive tracers, electrolytes and biomolecules; this lesson does not yet establish radioactive-decay equations or clinical electrolyte reference ranges.

## Short concept checks
- An oxygen nucleus has eight protons and ten neutrons. What are Z and A? **Answer:** Z is 8 and A is 18; the atom is oxygen-18.
- Does removing an electron from that atom create fluorine? **Answer:** No. It changes charge, while fluorine requires nine protons.

## Key takeaways
Element identity follows proton number. Isotope identity also requires neutron information; ion charge depends on electron balance. Biological explanation proceeds from composition to chemical form, organisation and regulated processes.
