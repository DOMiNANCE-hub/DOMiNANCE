# Multiple stereocentres, meso compounds and racemates

## Learning goals
Classify enantiomeric and diastereomeric relationships. Explain the 2ⁿ maximum, internal symmetry and the difference between an achiral meso compound and an optically inactive racemic mixture.

## Local centres and whole-molecule symmetry
Two molecules with the same connectivity may differ at one or more stereogenic elements. For a simple pair with multiple tetrahedral stereocentres and no complicating symmetry, inverting every centre produces the mirror-image relationship; changing some but not all produces diastereomers. Always check the whole structure, because symmetry can make apparently different labels describe an identical molecule.

With n independent tetrahedral stereocentres, 2ⁿ is a maximum number of configurations before accounting for equivalence and symmetry. It is not a universal exact count of all isomers for every molecule. Constitutional alternatives and additional double-bond stereochemistry are separate considerations. Two stereocentres suggest a maximum of four, but internal symmetry can reduce the distinct number.

Tartaric acid illustrates the distinction. The (2R,3R) and (2S,3S) forms are enantiomers. The internally symmetric (2R,3S) form is identical to the corresponding (2S,3R) representation and is meso: it has stereocentres but is achiral as a whole. Thus there are three distinct stereoisomers in this familiar example, not four.

## Worked racemate versus meso comparison
An equimolar mixture of two enantiomers is racemic. Under the same conditions, the opposite optical rotations of the equally represented enantiomers cancel in the bulk measurement. The mixture contains chiral molecules; zero net rotation does not make each molecule achiral.

A pure meso compound has zero ordinary optical rotation because the molecular structure is achiral. It is not a 50:50 mixture of two enantiomers and cannot be separated into those two supposed constituents by ordinary resolution. A flat zero-rotation reading alone therefore cannot distinguish a racemate from a meso compound or another achiral substance.

For an illustrative 75:25 mixture of two enantiomers, enantiomeric excess is |75 − 25| = 50%. If rotations add linearly under the stated conditions and the pure major enantiomer would rotate +10°, the mixture would rotate approximately +5°. The calculation needs the equal-and-opposite pure-rotation assumption and does not establish configuration or purity from a single unqualified reading.

## Why resolution can work
Enantiomers often have equal ordinary physical properties in achiral environments, but interact differently with chiral partners. A chiral stationary phase can retain them differently. Reaction with an appropriate single-enantiomer resolving agent can form diastereomeric derivatives or salts with differing physical properties, enabling separation and recovery. A nonchiral separation medium does not generally distinguish enantiomers solely because their structural drawings look different.

Biological systems can also discriminate, but the lecturer's specific microbial preference and nicotine-toxicity comparison require more contextual evidence before becoming scored facts. These notes teach the mechanism of chiral discrimination without inventing universal organism preferences or clinical safety rules.

## Short concept checks
- Must a molecule with two stereocentres have four distinct stereoisomers? **Answer:** No. Four is a maximum under the simple model; symmetry can produce equivalence and a meso form.
- Why is a racemate not the same thing as a meso compound? **Answer:** A racemate contains equal amounts of two chiral enantiomers; a meso compound is a single achiral molecular structure with stereocentres.

## Key takeaways
Count possible configurations, then test symmetry. Optical inactivity does not uniquely identify molecular structure or mixture composition. Chiral interactions explain both biological discrimination and resolution.
