# Allostery, cooperativity and feedback control

## Learning goals
Explain how ligand binding can regulate a distant active site, distinguish cooperativity from allostery and separate immediate product inhibition from pathway end-product feedback.

## Binding at one site can influence another
Allosteric regulation involves communication between distinct sites in a macromolecule. A regulatory ligand can favour conformational populations with different substrate affinity or catalytic activity. The effector does not need to resemble the substrate or chemically modify the enzyme. Noncovalent binding can provide rapid, reversible responses, but exact timescales depend on binding, conformational changes and cellular delivery.

The lecturer's “other site” model is useful for distinguishing an effector site from an active site. However, a regulatory effect is not automatically pure noncompetitive inhibition, and an allosteric activator need not simply increase one universal constant. It can affect affinity-like behaviour, catalytic capacity or both. Some allosteric proteins have multiple subunits; communication can also occur between sites in a single chain.

## Cooperative substrate responses require an appropriate model
Positive cooperativity means binding or reaction behaviour at one site influences other sites so that increasing substrate can produce a steeper response over a concentration range. A sigmoidal rate curve can illustrate such behaviour, but a sigmoid alone does not identify a unique structure or prove a particular number of sites. Negative cooperativity and more complex responses also occur.

A cooperative enzyme may not obey the simple Michaelis–Menten equation. An operational [S]0.5 can describe the substrate concentration giving half a specified limiting rate under the fitted conditions. It should not automatically be renamed Km or treated as an equilibrium dissociation constant. Even an empirical steepness coefficient is not automatically a subunit count.

## Feedback differs from merely approaching equilibrium
Product accumulation can decrease net forward rate because the reverse reaction becomes more important. That thermodynamic progress effect is distinct from product binding and kinetically inhibiting an enzyme. End-product feedback often describes a later product inhibiting an earlier pathway enzyme, sometimes at a committed branch step. The target enzyme need not directly produce the inhibitory end product.

For a hypothetical A→B→C→D pathway, D might bind a regulatory site on the A→B enzyme and reduce further input when D is abundant. Removing D can relieve that inhibition. This can conserve precursors and balance pathway output, but the feedback does not by itself change the equilibrium constant of A⇌B. Cellular flux still depends on supply, competing branches and other controlled steps.

## Worked interpretation of regulator data
Suppose a theoretical control curve reaches a limiting rate of 100 units and has half-rate substrate concentration 8 mM. In the presence of an activator it retains the same limiting rate but reaches half-rate at 2 mM. The observations support a shift in substrate responsiveness under the stated model; they do not show a fourfold increase in the limiting rate or prove the enzyme amount quadrupled.

If another effector lowers the limiting rate, both response position and capacity should be described rather than forcing every effect into a competitive-inhibitor category. Direct binding, structural changes and concentration-dependent kinetics would help distinguish mechanisms. These are constructed teaching values, not experimental measurements.

## Coordinating a pathway
Feedback is one control among many. Cells can alter enzyme abundance, localisation, covalent state, cofactors and partner proteins. Control can be distributed across several reactions; a single “rate-limiting enzyme” label should not be treated as an unchanging property independent of condition. Unit 9 develops pathway-level reasoning from these foundations.

## Short concept checks
- Must an end-product feedback inhibitor be the immediate product of the enzyme it inhibits? **Answer:** No. A later pathway product can regulate an earlier step.
- Does a sigmoid permit calling every half-rate midpoint Km? **Answer:** No. Use an appropriate model and an operational [S]0.5 when Michaelis–Menten behaviour is not established.

## Key takeaways
Allostery describes communication; cooperativity describes linked responses; feedback describes pathway organisation. None is interchangeable with changing a reaction's thermodynamic equilibrium constant.
