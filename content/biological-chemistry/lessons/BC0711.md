# Protein functions, conjugates and recognition

## Learning goals
Connect representative protein functions to structural features and distinguish chemical conjugate categories from physiological roles.

## The same chemical toolkit supports many roles
Protein side chains supply binding surfaces, catalytic groups and structural contacts. Enzymes accelerate reactions through favourable catalytic pathways rather than supplying unlimited free energy. Some enzymes act on several related substrates; “one enzyme can only ever catalyse one exact reaction under every condition” is too rigid. Catalytic RNA is an important exception to “all biological catalysts are proteins”, already introduced in Unit 6.

Regulatory proteins include peptide hormones such as insulin and proteins that influence gene expression or other proteins' activity. Not every hormone is a protein: steroid and thyroid hormones require separate chemical classification. Albumin transports several hydrophobic substances in plasma; haemoglobin binds oxygen. Transporters move particular solutes across membranes and do not all use the same mechanism or energy source.

Storage proteins can supply amino acids during development, as in egg or seed proteins. Ferritin instead stores iron within a protein assembly. Actin, myosin, tubulin and associated motors participate in cellular structure and movement, but they have different roles: actin and microtubules provide polymers/tracks; myosin and other motors convert chemical energy into mechanical work. Keratin, collagen and elastin support different material properties, not one interchangeable “structural protein” behaviour.

## Recognition makes assemblies selective
Antibodies bind particular molecular features and participate in defence. Recognition is selective, not a guarantee that every foreign molecule is neutralised or that self-reactivity never occurs. Fibrinogen and its conversion into a fibrin network contribute to clot formation; thrombin has a catalytic role. Protein functions can overlap rather than fit into mutually exclusive bins.

Scaffold and adapter proteins bring components together. A domain is a region with characteristic recognition properties: SH2 domains commonly recognise suitable phosphotyrosine-containing motifs; SH3 domains often recognise particular proline-rich sequences. The surrounding sequence and geometry matter, so an isolated phosphorylated tyrosine or any proline does not guarantee binding. Many PDZ domains recognise particular C-terminal sequences rather than merely the identity of a single final amino acid. PH-domain membrane interactions depend on the particular domain and lipid context.

## Conjugate classification asks what accompanies the protein
A conjugated protein contains a non-amino-acid component integrated into its functional molecular structure. A prosthetic group is a tightly associated nonprotein component important to function; it need not be covalently attached. Loosely exchanging substrates should not all be relabelled prosthetic groups. The historical “simple protein” category describes amino-acid-only composition, not an assertion that such a protein never binds an ion or nucleotide during activity.

| Category | Nonprotein component or association | Useful example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glycoprotein | Carbohydrate | Many extracellular and cell-surface proteins |
| Lipoprotein | Lipid-containing complex | Plasma particles transporting lipids |
| Nucleoprotein | Nucleic-acid complex | Ribosome or chromatin |
| Metalloprotein | Associated metal | Ferritin and many metal-dependent enzymes |
| Haemoprotein | Iron-containing haem | Haemoglobin, cytochromes, catalase |
| Phosphoprotein | Covalently attached phosphate | Casein or a regulated phosphoprotein |
| Flavoprotein | Flavin cofactor, FMN or FAD | Several redox enzymes |

These categories can overlap. A haemoprotein is also a metalloprotein, but not every metalloprotein contains haem. A glycoprotein may also carry phosphate. Different chemical categories do not prescribe an identical location or task.

## Worked classification without overreach
A protein that contains haem and catalyses peroxide breakdown can be both a haemoprotein and an enzyme. Its haem classification concerns composition; its enzyme classification concerns function. A plasma lipoprotein particle transports lipids in an aqueous environment but does not prove that its lipid component is covalently attached to every apoprotein. Identifying a category does not establish a patient's disease risk from an isolated observation.

## Short concept checks
- Are all metalloproteins haemoproteins? **Answer:** No. Haemoproteins contain a particular iron–porphyrin group; other metalloproteins use different metal associations.
- Does every protein hormone mean every hormone is a protein? **Answer:** No. Insulin is a protein hormone, while other hormone classes have different chemistry.

## Key takeaways
Keep function, shape and composition as separate classification axes. Recognition depends on a specific molecular context, and categories can overlap.
