# Hormonal coordination of metabolism across cells

## Learning goals
Explain receptor-dependent hormonal effects, compare representative intracellular-receptor and surface-receptor routes and qualify the glucagon/cAMP glycogen example.

## A circulating signal requires a responsive target
An endocrine hormone is released from specialised cells and can reach distant tissues through the circulation. A target response depends on receptor expression and the downstream machinery present, not merely on exposure to the same blood. Different tissues can therefore respond differently to one hormone. Hormones regulate cellular pathways; they are not necessarily consumed as substrates by every enzyme whose rate changes.

A first messenger is the external signal in a surface-receptor model. A second messenger is an intracellular mediator generated or changed downstream. Amplification can occur because one activated component influences several downstream molecules, but finite substrates, receptor regulation and signal removal limit the response.

## Intracellular receptors and gene expression
Many steroid hormones can cross membranes and interact with intracellular receptors that regulate gene expression. Some receptors are already substantially nuclear; others change localisation. The source's sequence of every receptor first binding in cytoplasm and then moving to the nucleus is a useful schematic for some cases, not a universal trajectory for all steroid receptors.

Hormone–receptor complexes can recruit regulatory partners and alter transcription of selected genes. More transcript does not instantaneously guarantee a proportional new enzyme or flux: translation, turnover, substrates and competing regulation intervene. Steroids can also have more rapid signalling effects, so “steroid means only slow transcription and never anything else” is too absolute. Nonsteroid hormones do not all signal via cAMP; insulin and thyroid hormone illustrate why chemistry and receptor mechanism must be specified separately.

## A qualified glucagon–cAMP pathway
In a familiar hepatic model, glucagon binds a surface G-protein-coupled receptor. G-protein activation can stimulate adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cAMP. cAMP promotes protein kinase A activity, leading through regulated phosphorylation steps to changes favouring glycogen mobilisation and reduced glycogen synthesis. The hormone does not need to enter the cell and directly phosphorylate glycogen.

This example concerns a receptor-responsive tissue, especially liver; it must not become a universal identical response in every glycogen-containing cell. Receptor distribution, available glycogen, nutritional state and other controls matter. cAMP is a messenger, not the phosphate donor used by PKA on its protein targets; ATP supplies that phosphoryl group in the kinase reaction.

## Turning a signal off is part of the mechanism
G-protein GTP hydrolysis, receptor regulation, phosphodiesterase-mediated conversion of cAMP and protein dephosphorylation help terminate or reshape signalling. Their regulation prevents a brief external signal from necessarily causing unlimited persistent output. At the gene-expression level, changing transcript and protein lifetimes adds another timescale.

In a theoretical example, a signal raises cAMP and downstream enzyme phosphorylation while total enzyme protein remains unchanged during a short observation. That is consistent with regulation of existing proteins, not proof of new gene expression. If transcript and protein abundance rise later, the mechanisms can coexist rather than being competing explanations from which only one is allowed.

## Biological application without prescribing
Hormonal coordination helps tissues handle changing fuel supply and demand. A response measured in one isolated preparation does not establish another tissue's response or a patient's diagnosis. The diagrams and examples here teach causal steps and limitations; no hormone intervention, dose, patient measurement or clinical outcome was tested.

## Short concept checks
- Does cAMP replace ATP as PKA's ordinary protein-phosphorylation donor? **Answer:** No. cAMP is a regulatory messenger; ATP provides the transferred phosphoryl group.
- Why can two tissues exposed to glucagon respond differently? **Answer:** Receptor expression, downstream machinery, available stores and other conditions differ.

## Key takeaways
Follow the signal from receptor to mediator to effector and termination. Tissue context and overlapping rapid/slow mechanisms are essential qualifications.
