# Peptide formation, direction and backbone geometry

## Learning goals
Identify the peptide bond and termini, explain why activation is required in cells, and distinguish restricted amide rotation from the conformational freedom of adjacent backbone bonds.

## A covalent amide creates a directional chain
An ordinary peptide bond joins the α-carboxyl-derived carbonyl carbon of one residue to the α-amino-derived nitrogen of the next: –C(=O)–N–. A simple formal condensation can be balanced as two free amino acids giving a dipeptide plus water. This is chemical bookkeeping, not a complete mechanism of ribosomal synthesis. In cells amino acids are activated and attached to tRNAs using energy derived from ATP; the ribosome catalyses transfer from an activated peptidyl-tRNA ester to the incoming amino group. Mixing free amino acids in water is not an adequate spontaneous protein-synthesis process.

For the usual unmodified linear chain, the end with a free α-amino group is the N terminus and the end with a free α-carboxyl group is the C terminus. Sequences are written N→C unless explicitly stated otherwise. Gly–Ala means glycine at the N end and alanine at the C end; Ala–Gly has the same composition but a different sequence. NH3+ or COO− terminal drawings depend on pH. Blocking, cyclisation and unusual isopeptide linkages require explicit exceptions.

## Resonance constrains the peptide unit
The nitrogen lone pair can delocalise into the carbonyl system. The peptide C–N bond therefore has partial double-bond character and is relatively rigid and planar. The amide nitrogen is not simply a free amino group with the same basicity. The peptide carbonyl oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond; an ordinary peptide N–H can donate one. Internal proline lacks that N–H donor.

Most peptide units are trans: neighbouring α carbons lie on opposite sides of the peptide bond, reducing steric crowding. Cis units occur and are relatively more frequent before proline, but a proline-containing bond is not automatically cis. Rotation about adjacent N–Cα and Cα–C(=O) single bonds, conventionally φ and ψ, changes backbone conformation. Not all angle combinations are allowed because atoms would collide. Glycine has fewer side-chain restrictions; proline's ring restricts its φ range. A planar peptide unit can therefore belong to an overall curved or folded chain.

## Worked bond and terminus inventory
Consider the unmodified linear tripeptide Gly–Ala–Ser. There are three residues and two peptide bonds. The internal alanine has neither a free α-amino nor a free α-carboxyl group. The chain has one N terminus and one C terminus, plus serine's side-chain OH. Counting three free amino groups would ignore incorporation into amides.

If this chain is completely hydrolysed under suitable conditions, two backbone linkages are cleaved with net consumption of two waters, producing three free amino acids. Partial hydrolysis yields fragments and may preserve order information. Disulfide reduction is different: it can separate linked regions or chains without cleaving these peptide bonds.

## Short concept checks
- Which connection is the peptide bond: Cα–Cα or carbonyl C–N? **Answer:** The carbonyl carbon-to-nitrogen amide connection, not a direct bond between adjacent α carbons.
- Can a folded protein contain individually planar peptide units? **Answer:** Yes. Rotation about permitted adjacent backbone bonds changes the orientation of successive planar units.

## Key takeaways
Sequence has direction. Explain both activated biological synthesis and the formal condensation balance; keep peptide-bond rigidity separate from whole-chain folding.
