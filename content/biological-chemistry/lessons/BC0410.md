# Chirality, R/S configuration and optical rotation

## Learning goals
Recognise nonsuperimposable mirror images. Apply introductory R/S priorities with an explicit viewing direction and distinguish absolute configuration, D/L notation and measured optical rotation.

## A molecule can have handedness
A chiral object is not superimposable on its mirror image. A common molecular cause is a tetrahedral carbon attached to four different groups. The whole group must be compared: two substituents beginning with carbon can still be different farther along their chains. A molecule with two identical substituents at a particular tetrahedral carbon does not have a conventional stereocentre at that carbon. More complex sources of chirality exist, so this common structural test is not an exhaustive definition of all chiral molecules.

Enantiomers are nonsuperimposable mirror-image stereoisomers. Opposite drawings that can be superimposed by rotation represent the same object, not an enantiomer pair. Biological binding sites are themselves chiral, so enantiomers can interact differently with enzymes and receptors. That possibility does not justify claiming that one member of every pair is beneficial and the other harmful.

## Worked R/S assignment
Rank the four attached groups by CIP rules. Directly attached Br outranks Cl, which outranks F, which outranks H. Orient the lowest-priority group, H, away from the observer. Follow 1 → 2 → 3: clockwise gives R, counterclockwise gives S. If the lowest-priority group points toward the observer instead, reverse the apparent clockwise/counterclockwise result. If it lies in the drawing plane, first reorient the molecule consistently rather than guessing.

A solid wedge represents a bond toward the observer; a hashed wedge represents a bond away. Ordinary lines lie approximately in the drawing plane. Projection conventions provide information about the third dimension; they are not decorative styles.

For lactic acid, CH₃–CH(OH)–COOH, OH ranks above the two carbon-based groups because O outranks C. To break the carbon tie, the carboxyl carbon's attached-atom comparison outranks the methyl carbon's hydrogens, using the multiple-bond convention. The order is OH, COOH, CH₃, H. An R/S descriptor still requires the actual spatial arrangement, not just this priority list.

## Three labels that must remain separate
R/S describes absolute configuration by priority and spatial arrangement. D/L expresses a configurational relationship to the glyceraldehyde reference in appropriate conventions; it is particularly useful for sugars and amino acids. Neither system alone predicts the sign of optical rotation.

The measured signs (+) and (−) describe clockwise or anticlockwise rotation of plane-polarised light under specified experimental conditions. Wavelength, temperature, solvent, concentration and path length can matter when reporting rotation. A positive rotation is not a universal synonym for R or D. A hydroxyl group on the right of a Fischer drawing does not by itself prove dextrorotation.

## Fischer projections and source correction
In a Fischer projection, horizontal bonds point toward the observer and vertical bonds point away. The carbon chain is conventionally arranged vertically. A 180° rotation in the drawing plane preserves the configuration, while a 90° rotation changes the projection's stereochemical meaning unless an appropriate transformation is accounted for.

## Short concept checks
- Can the label R alone tell whether a pure enantiomer is (+) or (−)? **Answer:** No. Absolute configuration and measured optical rotation are different descriptors.
- With group 4 away, does a clockwise 1 → 2 → 3 sequence give R or S? **Answer:** R; if group 4 were toward the observer, the apparent result would be reversed.

## Key takeaways
Determine molecular handedness and use explicit projection rules. Keep R/S, D/L and optical rotation separate; a structural label is not a measurement of light rotation or biological activity.
