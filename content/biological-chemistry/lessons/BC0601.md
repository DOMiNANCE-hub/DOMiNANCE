# Bases, nucleosides and nucleotides

## Learning goals
Identify canonical purines and pyrimidines, distinguish bases from nucleosides and nucleotides, and use sugar/base numbering to locate their bonds.

## Build the molecule in three stages
A nitrogenous base is a heterocyclic ring system containing nitrogen. Adenine and guanine are purines with fused six- and five-membered rings. Cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines with one six-membered ring. The number of nitrogen atoms is not the same as the number of rings. Thymine is 5-methyluracil; its methyl group changes the base without making it a purine.

A nucleoside consists of base plus sugar. Adding one or more phosphate groups gives a nucleotide. Adenine is therefore not synonymous with adenosine or AMP. In the canonical nucleosides, the sugar's anomeric C1′ is joined through a β N-glycosidic bond to purine N9 or pyrimidine N1. Prime marks distinguish sugar positions from unprimed base-ring numbers. C1′ and base N1 are different atoms even when their names contain the same digit.

## Ribose, deoxyribose and naming
RNA's usual sugar is ribose, with a C2′ OH. DNA uses 2′-deoxyribose, with H at that position. Both sugars retain C3′ OH, crucial to ordinary chain extension. Losing 2′ OH does not mean losing every sugar oxygen or the 3′ group.

| Base | Ribose nucleoside | Representative nucleotide |
|---|---|---|
| Adenine | Adenosine | AMP, adenosine monophosphate |
| Guanine | Guanosine | GMP |
| Cytosine | Cytidine | CMP |
| Uracil | Uridine | UMP |

Deoxy forms need their appropriate prefix, such as deoxyadenosine and dAMP. Thymidine conventionally often denotes deoxythymidine in DNA contexts; ribothymidine is a distinct modified-RNA example. State sugar identity rather than inferring it only from a familiar base. The lecturer illustrates 5′ and 3′ monophosphates; the position belongs in a precise name when alternatives matter.

## Read the base functional groups
Adenine is 6-aminopurine; guanine has an amino group at C2 and an oxo group at C6 in the usual representation. Cytosine has C4 amino and C2 oxo groups, while uracil has oxo groups at C2 and C4. Thymine adds a C5 methyl group to the uracil framework. These unprimed ring positions are different from the sugar positions.

Amino and oxo groups provide hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor patterns important to pairing. Dominant tautomeric forms and protonation matter: changing the position of a proton and double bonds can alter that pattern without adding a whole new sugar. The canonical drawings are useful references, not a claim that every base has only one possible protonation state under all conditions.

## Worked structural diagnosis
A molecule containing adenine and ribose but no phosphorus is adenosine, a nucleoside. Adding a phosphate ester at C5′ gives adenosine 5′-monophosphate. Adding linked phosphates yields ADP or ATP, not extra bases or extra sugar units. Conversely, AMP hydrolysis that removes phosphate need not break the base–sugar bond; those are different chemical linkages.

## Short concept checks
- Does guanine alone count as a nucleotide? **Answer:** No. It is a base; a nucleotide also contains sugar and phosphate.
- Which base atom normally joins sugar C1′ in a canonical pyrimidine nucleoside? **Answer:** N1; canonical purines use N9.

## Key takeaways
Identify base, sugar and phosphate separately. Use prime numbering and bond type to explain what a reaction changes.
