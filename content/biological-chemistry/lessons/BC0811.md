# Irreversible and mechanism-based enzyme inactivation

## Learning goals
Distinguish persistent inactivation from reversible inhibition, explain a mechanism-based inhibitor and evaluate recovery or time-dependence experiments without overinterpreting one observation.

## Loss of active enzyme is a different process
A persistent inhibitor can reduce the concentration of functioning catalytic centres. Covalent modification is a common mechanism of irreversible inhibition, but irreversible behaviour is not defined solely by the presence of a covalent bond. Conversely, a covalent intermediate in an ordinary catalytic cycle can be transient and productive. Duration, recovery and the reaction mechanism matter.

If half the active centres are inactivated while the remaining enzyme is unchanged, the simple prediction is half the original Vmax at the same assay conditions. The Km of surviving centres need not change. This may resemble a pure noncompetitive rate pattern, but it does not establish the same reversible-binding mechanism.

## Time and recovery provide complementary evidence
In a conceptual preincubation experiment, measure activity after different enzyme–inhibitor exposure times, then assay under common conditions. Increasing inhibition with exposure can support a time-dependent process. Dilution or removal of free inhibitor may permit recovery of a reversible complex, while a stable covalent adduct can persist. Very slow dissociation, tight binding, irreversible denaturation or assay interference can complicate these observations.

Therefore, lack of immediate recovery after one dilution is not absolute proof of a specific covalent attachment. Direct adduct detection, suitable controls and longer recovery measurements may be needed. A modification that blocks activity could alter substrate access or folding rather than uniquely identify the residue that performs the chemical step.

## Mechanism-based inactivation uses catalytic chemistry
A mechanism-based inhibitor is processed by the target enzyme so that reactive chemistry generated during turnover contributes to inactivation. It differs from any indiscriminate electrophile that reacts with many proteins without enzyme-mediated activation. Specificity can arise from recognition and catalytic processing, but neither zero off-target action nor universally high affinity follows merely from the label.

The lecturer's antibiotic example concerns β-lactam reaction with susceptible bacterial penicillin-binding proteins, including transpeptidase activities involved in peptidoglycan cross-linking. Correct the vague “glycoprotein peptidase” wording to that supported target description. A persistent acyl-enzyme interaction can block a required cell-wall synthetic step. This is different from acting on a human peptide-digesting enzyme or directly cleaving all existing bacterial wall glycan bonds.

This mechanism does not prove every bacterium is susceptible: access barriers, β-lactamases and altered targets can matter. The example teaches target chemistry; it supplies no prescribing, dosing or treatment-selection rule. More specific clinical claims require their own reliable evidence and human review.

## Worked active-fraction interpretation
Suppose original Vmax is 80 µM/min and an independently established irreversible treatment leaves 25% of centres active. Under a model where surviving centres are unchanged and substrate can saturate them, predicted Vmax is 80×0.25=20 µM/min. This is not evidence that Km also became one quarter or that enzyme concentration increased.

If an experiment instead shows reduced rate plus a changed substrate dependence, simple removal of equivalent active centres may be inadequate; altered enzyme forms or mixed effects should be considered. Numerical resemblance to a textbook curve is not sufficient to assert one molecular mechanism.

## Short concept checks
- Is every temporary covalent enzyme intermediate an irreversible inhibitor? **Answer:** No. Productive cycles can form and then resolve covalent intermediates, regenerating the enzyme.
- Does failure to recover instantly after dilution prove a particular covalent adduct? **Answer:** No. Slow dissociation, instability and other explanations require controls and further evidence.

## Key takeaways
Inactivation changes the functioning enzyme population. Use time, recovery and chemical evidence together; a kinetic pattern alone cannot identify every mechanism.
