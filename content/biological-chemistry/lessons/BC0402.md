# Covalent structures, octets and formal charge

## Learning goals
Build and check simple Lewis structures. Distinguish electron ownership bookkeeping from the actual distribution of electron density, and recognise octet exceptions without inventing unsupported orbital mechanisms.

## Sharing and counting electrons
A covalent bond involves shared electron density between atoms. A Lewis structure represents valence electrons as bonding pairs and lone pairs. Start by counting all valence electrons, adding one for each negative charge and subtracting one for each positive charge. Choose a reasonable connectivity, place single bonds, complete terminal-atom shells where appropriate and allocate the remaining electrons. If a central period-2 atom lacks an octet, consider multiple bonding rather than adding electrons that are not available.

Hydrogen normally has a duet rather than an octet. Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine often satisfy an octet in introductory structures. The rule is useful but not universal: BF₃ is electron deficient, odd-electron species need separate treatment, and some conventional Lewis drawings for heavier central atoms show more than eight electrons. Such drawings do not prove that expanded octets arise from large d-orbital participation; that historical explanation is an oversimplification.

Formal charge is a bookkeeping quantity: valence electrons of the free atom minus nonbonding electrons minus half the bonding electrons. Equivalently, subtract the number of bonds counted by bond order and the number of lone-pair electrons from the valence count. The sum of formal charges must equal the species' net charge. Formal charge is not identical to partial charge or a direct measurement of where all electron density lies.

## Worked carbon dioxide and ammonium
CO₂ has 4 + 2×6 = 16 valence electrons. An O–C–O skeleton uses four in two single bonds. Completing the oxygen octets with the remaining twelve leaves carbon short of an octet. Convert one lone pair on each oxygen into an additional shared pair: O=C=O. Each oxygen retains two lone pairs, carbon has four bond-order units and the total remains sixteen. The main Lewis structure has zero formal charge on each atom.

NH₄+ has 5 + 4×1 − 1 = 8 valence electrons. Four N–H single bonds use all eight. Nitrogen's formal charge is 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. The positive ion has no nitrogen lone pair in this structure. Failing to subtract one electron for the charge would give an impossible odd total for the intended closed-shell drawing.

For a species that needs several equivalent resonance contributors, the actual structure is not a molecule rapidly switching between separate drawings. Contributors are alternative electron-bookkeeping representations of a delocalised state. Keep atom connectivity fixed when comparing resonance contributors; moving an atom or proton can instead create an isomer or tautomer.

## Misconceptions and application
An octet is not a statement that every atom has eight total electrons: oxygen's inner 1s electrons are distinct from its valence count. Four bonds around carbon can include double bonds, and lone pairs contribute to electron counting and geometry even though they are not drawn as connections to another nucleus. Charged functional groups in biological molecules make correct charge bookkeeping particularly useful.

## Short concept checks
- How many valence electrons are counted for NH₄+? **Answer:** Eight: 5 + 4 − 1.
- Is nitrogen in NH₄+ assigned a lone pair by that Lewis structure? **Answer:** No. The four N–H bonds use the eight available electrons; nitrogen carries formal charge +1.

## Key takeaways
Count the total first, preserve it through every drawing change and check charge as well as octets. Lewis structures are explanatory models whose limits must remain explicit.
