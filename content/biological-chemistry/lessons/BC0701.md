# Amino-acid architecture and stereochemistry

## Learning goals
Identify the common backbone and side chain of an α-amino acid. Distinguish a free amino acid from a residue, explain glycine and proline exceptions, and interpret L/D notation without confusing it with optical rotation.

## One backbone, different chemical possibilities
A typical free α-amino acid has an α carbon attached to an amino group, a carboxyl group, hydrogen and a variable R group. The α designation locates that carbon next to the carboxyl carbon; it does not refer to an α helix. In near-neutral water, the carboxyl group is usually COO− and the amino group usually NH3+. This zwitterion has both positive and negative groups. A drawing of NH2–CH(R)–COOH is useful for connectivity, but does not automatically show the dominant ionisation state at a stated pH.

The shared backbone permits polymer formation, while side chains provide differences in charge, hydrogen bonding, size, hydrophobicity and reactivity. A side-chain hydroxyl is chemically different from an alcohol-free hydrocarbon, and a carboxamide differs from a carboxylate. These differences help determine folding, binding and catalysis. They are not merely names to memorise.

A residue is an amino-acid unit incorporated into a peptide. For an internal ordinary residue, its α-amino and α-carboxyl groups participate in backbone amide linkages. Consequently, a protein is not a row of independent free zwitterions. The terminal groups and ionisable side chains contribute much of its pH-dependent charge.

## Two important structural exceptions
Glycine has R=H, so the α carbon bears two identical hydrogens and is achiral. Its small side chain permits backbone conformations sterically difficult for many other residues. Flexibility can help tight turns, but does not mean glycine always stabilises a regular helix.

Proline has a side chain that reconnects to its amino nitrogen. The free amino acid therefore contains a secondary amine, not an absent amino group or an absent carboxyl group. The ring restricts backbone rotation. Once incorporated internally, its peptide nitrogen lacks an N–H donor. Both geometry and loss of that donor can disrupt a regular helix. Proline may occur at turns or particular helix positions; it is not forbidden from every protein helix.

## Reading configuration correctly
Most ordinary chiral residues inserted by ribosomal protein synthesis have the L configuration. In a standard amino-acid Fischer projection with carboxyl above and R below, amino on the left indicates L; horizontal bonds point towards the viewer. Rotation or redrawing must preserve the stereochemical convention. Glycine has no L/D enantiomers. D amino acids do exist biologically, including in bacterial cell-wall peptides, so “all biological amino acids are L” is too broad.

L/D compares configuration with a reference convention; it does not specify the measured direction of optical rotation. Nor is L identical to S: most common L α-amino acids are S, but L-cysteine is R because sulfur changes the priority ranking. Isoleucine and threonine each have a second stereocentre, so specify the complete structure when distinguishing their stereoisomers. Unit 4 explains the underlying priority method.

## Worked classification by connectivity
Given H3N+–CH(CH2OH)–COO−, identify the α carbon as CH, the side chain as CH2OH and the residue type as serine. The OH belongs to the side chain, not to the backbone carboxylate. Replacing CH2OH with H gives glycine and removes α-carbon chirality; replacing it with CH3 gives alanine and retains a stereocentre.

## Short concept checks
- Does a protein contain a free α-amino group on every internal residue? **Answer:** No. Most internal α-amino groups form peptide amide linkages; the N terminus and appropriate side chains must be considered separately.
- Why does free proline still count as an amino acid? **Answer:** It retains a carboxyl group and an amino nitrogen, but that nitrogen is a secondary amine in a ring.

## Key takeaways
Read connectivity, ionisation and stereochemistry separately. Glycine is achiral; proline is cyclic; L/D does not predict optical rotation or universally equal S/R.
