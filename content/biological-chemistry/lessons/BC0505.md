# Deoxy, amino, phosphate and glycoside derivatives

## Learning goals
Identify important sugar substitutions and explain how they change charge, recognition or reactivity. Distinguish a phosphate ester, bisphosphate, diphosphate and an anomeric glycoside.

## Replacing an OH or adding an amino group
In 2-deoxyribose, the C2 OH of ribose is replaced by H. This removes one oxygen overall; it does not remove the carbon or strip all OH groups from the sugar. DNA contains 2-deoxyribose while RNA contains ribose. Fucose and rhamnose are other biologically useful deoxy-sugar examples. The position number is essential because different OH replacements would give different derivatives.

An amino sugar replaces an OH with an amino group, as in glucosamine or galactosamine. Acetylation of that nitrogen gives an N-acetyl derivative such as N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc). This changes the nitrogen's chemistry without making it the same as the original OH. Such residues occur in cell-surface glycans and structural polymers; chitin contains GlcNAc, while bacterial peptidoglycan includes GlcNAc and N-acetylmuramic acid.

## Phosphate position and phosphate connectivity
A sugar OH can form a phosphate ester. Glucose 1-phosphate and glucose 6-phosphate differ in the carbon bearing phosphate. The added ionisable phosphate makes the derivative more charged and changes enzyme recognition; it should not be treated as chemically identical to free glucose. Passive passage through a lipid membrane is generally less favourable for charged derivatives.

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate has two separate phosphate ester groups at C1 and C6. Bisphosphate therefore differs from a diphosphate group containing a P–O–P connection. ATP has a ribose-to-phosphate ester and linked phosphates; its triphosphate chain is not three independent sugar OH substitutions. Read the connectivity, not just the count of phosphorus atoms.

## Turning a hemiacetal into a glycoside
At a free anomeric centre a cyclic sugar bears OH and ring OR. Replacing the anomeric OH through reaction with an alcohol gives an O-glycoside with two OR linkages at that centre: an acetal or ketal. Methyl α-D-glucoside has a methyl group connected through oxygen at the anomeric centre; it is not glucose with a methyl attached arbitrarily to a carbon in the backbone.

A full glycoside does not undergo ordinary mutarotation at its locked anomeric centre without cleavage. For the O-glycosides described here, appropriate acid or enzyme-catalysed hydrolysis can restore a free anomeric OH. Biological glycosidic linkages can also involve nitrogen, as in nucleosides; the word glycoside does not always mean a bond between two sugar molecules.

## Short concept checks
- Does fructose 1,6-bisphosphate contain one obligatory phosphate-to-phosphate bond? **Answer:** No. Its name specifies separate phosphate groups at two sugar positions, unlike a diphosphate chain.
- Why does methyl glucoside behave differently from free glucose at its anomeric centre? **Answer:** The anomeric OH has become a glycosidic acetal; ordinary ring opening and anomeric equilibration are blocked until cleavage.

## Key takeaways
Substitution position and bond connectivity determine a derivative's properties. Similar names and shared sugar backbones do not guarantee the same charge, enzyme recognition or reducing behaviour.
