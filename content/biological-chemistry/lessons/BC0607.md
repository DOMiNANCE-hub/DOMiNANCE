# Base reactions and RNA backbone susceptibility

## Learning goals
Explain why RNA is particularly susceptible to base-catalysed cleavage and distinguish denaturation, backbone cleavage, glycosidic cleavage and base modification.

## The neighbouring 2′ OH explains an important difference
RNA contains a 2′ hydroxyl next to the 3′–5′ backbone linkage. Under alkaline conditions it can be deprotonated, creating a nucleophilic 2′ oxygen. Intramolecular attack on the adjacent backbone phosphorus can form a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and cleave the chain, leaving a 5′ OH on the departing fragment. Subsequent hydrolysis can yield 2′- or 3′-phosphate products.

DNA lacks that 2′ OH, so it lacks this particular neighbouring-group pathway and is comparatively resistant under those conditions. It is not indestructible. Strong acid, enzymes, oxidation, radiation and other processes can damage DNA through different chemistry. A comparison of one mechanism should not become a claim that all DNA always outlasts every folded or protected RNA in every environment.

## Four different meanings of a structural change
Denaturation disrupts noncovalent pairing/organisation without necessarily breaking a chain. Backbone cleavage breaks a phosphodiester connection. Depurination can remove a purine by cleaving its sugar–base glycosidic bond, leaving an abasic site without initially severing that backbone. Deamination changes a base's functional groups and pairing tendencies. These outcomes require different analysis even though all can alter biological function.

Adenine deamination gives hypoxanthine and guanine deamination gives xanthine in the source's base-chemistry comparison. Cytosine deamination can generate uracil; cellular repair systems distinguish and process damage in context. Modified bases can also arise through regulated enzymatic processes, so a chemically modified base is not automatically a mutation or a harmful lesion.

## Sequence and environment affect reactions
Base rings contain positions with different electron densities, influencing addition/substitution chemistry. The source marks pyrimidine C2,C4,C6 and purine C2,C6,C8 as electron-deficient positions, but actual reactivity depends on substituents, reagent and molecular environment. A base buried in a paired/stacked region need not react at the same rate as the isolated base. Merely listing a ring position does not establish a universal ranking under all conditions.

This is why pH-driven strand separation and chemical hydrolysis must be distinguished. A duplex can become single-stranded while covalent chains remain intact; an intact-looking average absorbance can also conceal particular chemical lesions. No clinical mutation risk or complete sample quality is inferred from one bulk observation.

## Short concept checks
- Which RNA feature supplies the neighbouring nucleophile in the alkaline cleavage mechanism? **Answer:** The 2′ OH after deprotonation, enabling attack on the adjacent backbone phosphate.
- Does loss of a purine necessarily mean the phosphodiester chain was immediately broken? **Answer:** No. Depurination concerns the sugar–base bond and can first leave an abasic site on an otherwise continuous backbone.

## Key takeaways
Name the bond or interaction that changes. RNA's 2′ OH explains a specific susceptibility, not a universal guarantee of DNA stability or RNA instability.
