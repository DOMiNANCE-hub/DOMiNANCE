# Disaccharides: structure, digestion and comparison

## Learning goals
Compare maltose, lactose, sucrose, cellobiose and isomaltose. Explain why linkage recognition changes digestibility and why lactose intolerance differs from milk allergy or galactosaemia.

## A structured comparison
| Disaccharide | Units and main linkage | Free anomeric centre? | Key distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maltose | Glcα(1→4)Glc | Yes | Can arise during starch breakdown |
| Lactose | Galβ(1→4)Glc | Yes | Milk sugar; hydrolysed by lactase |
| Sucrose | Glcα(1↔2)βFru | No | Both anomeric centres participate |
| Cellobiose | Glcβ(1→4)Glc | Yes | Cellulose-related disaccharide |
| Isomaltose | Glcα(1→6)Glc | Yes | Illustrates a branch-type linkage |

Maltose and cellobiose contain the same sugar units and bond positions but differ in the donor's anomeric configuration. An enzyme's active site recognises three-dimensional geometry, so an α-linkage-cleaving enzyme cannot be assumed to cleave the corresponding β link. Isomaltose differs from maltose in linkage position rather than becoming a fructose-containing sugar.

## Digestion produces absorbable units
Dietary disaccharides are normally hydrolysed before their constituent monosaccharides are absorbed. Lactase releases glucose and galactose; sucrase releases glucose and fructose. Starch digestion produces maltose and other oligosaccharides that undergo further brush-border digestion. An enzyme name and its substrate bond matter: free glucose is not produced merely by declaring every glycosidic bond equivalent.

Low intestinal lactase activity leaves more lactose in the lumen. Unabsorbed solute draws water osmotically, and microbial metabolism contributes gas and organic products; this explains symptoms without treating them as proof of an immune allergy. Symptom severity depends on dose and context, and dietary approaches require individual assessment. Milk-protein allergy involves immune recognition, while galactosaemia involves metabolic handling of galactose: the three conditions are not interchangeable.

## Worked sucrose hydrolysis interpretation
Before hydrolysis, an intact sucrose sample has neither anomeric centre free. After suitable hydrolysis, glucose and fructose are released and can give a positive reducing-sugar test. The observation supports a change in the available reducing chemistry; it does not prove that untreated sucrose was a reducing sugar or that the mixture contains glucose alone. A paired untreated sample and appropriate controls help establish the difference.

Sucrose's high-concentration preservative effect also depends on concentration and water availability. Merely containing sucrose does not sterilise a food, and the lecture's dietary associations do not establish that a single exposure inevitably causes a chronic disease.

## Short concept checks
- Why can an α-amylase fail to digest cellulose even though cellulose contains glucose? **Answer:** Enzymes recognise linkage geometry; cellulose has β(1→4) bonds rather than the α(1→4) pattern recognised by α-amylase.
- Why can hydrolysed sucrose yield a positive reducing test? **Answer:** Cleavage restores free anomeric chemistry in glucose and fructose, unlike the locked intact disaccharide.

## Key takeaways
Compare identity, position and configuration separately. Digestibility, reducing behaviour, immunological reactions and nutritional context are different properties.
