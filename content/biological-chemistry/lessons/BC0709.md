# Tertiary folding, domains and denaturation

## Learning goals
Explain how a chain forms a functional fold, distinguish structural domains from subunits and compare denaturation, peptide hydrolysis and disulfide reduction.

## Tertiary structure integrates one chain
Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional arrangement of a polypeptide chain, including the relationships between its helices, sheets, loops and side chains. Distant sequence positions can become neighbours. A domain is a compact structural or functional region within a protein; a chain may contain several domains. It is not automatically a separate polypeptide subunit. A motif is a recurring arrangement or sequence pattern, often smaller than a domain, and need not fold independently.

Sequence contributes chemical constraints and interaction possibilities, but the realised structure also depends on solvent, temperature, pH, ligands, modifications and cellular assistance. Some regions are intrinsically disordered or change conformation during function. It is misleading to imagine every protein as a completely rigid sphere with one permanently frozen structure.

## Why a soluble chain often buries nonpolar groups
Water hydrogen-bonds extensively with itself. Exposing large nonpolar surfaces alters the surrounding solvent organisation. Clustering nonpolar side chains can reduce that exposure and favour folding: the hydrophobic effect is not an additional covalent bond between hydrocarbon groups. Close packing and van der Waals contacts help stabilise suitable arrangements, while hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions and disulfides can contribute in particular contexts.

A buried polar group often needs an appropriate partner, since losing favourable solvent interactions without replacement can be costly. A salt bridge depends on protonation and screening. A disulfide is a covalent linkage between selected cysteines, relatively common in extracellular proteins, not a universal feature of all cytosolic enzymes. Multiple interactions and their entropic costs determine net stability; a single attractive contact does not by itself explain the whole fold.

## Membrane and soluble proteins have different surroundings
Soluble globular proteins often enrich hydrophobic groups internally and hydrophilic groups at the aqueous surface, while retaining necessary pockets and mixed surfaces. In membrane-spanning regions, lipid-facing side chains are commonly hydrophobic, whereas an aqueous channel can have polar residues facing its lumen. Exposed extracellular and intracellular domains still interact with water. Thus, a membrane protein is not uniformly nonpolar everywhere.

Detergents can surround suitable hydrophobic surfaces and help solubilise some membrane proteins, but extraction can also disturb native complexes. Fibrous proteins often form elongated assemblies suited to strength or elasticity. Solubility and shape categories overlap with function; none is an infallible diagnostic rule.

## Denaturation is not the same as digestion
Heat, extreme pH or chemicals can disrupt noncovalent interactions and alter folding, often reducing function. Ordinary denaturation can preserve the backbone sequence while changing secondary, tertiary and quaternary organisation. Hydrolysis cleaves peptide bonds and changes chain connectivity. A reducing reagent can cleave disulfides without necessarily hydrolysing the peptide backbone. A treatment can cause more than one process, so specify its chemistry rather than assuming the words are interchangeable.

Some proteins can refold when suitable conditions are restored, illustrating information carried in sequence. Others aggregate, are chemically damaged or require partners and chaperones. Chaperones can assist productive folding or prevent inappropriate interactions; they do not ordinarily rewrite the encoded sequence. Loss of soluble protein does not establish whether the backbone was cut.

## Worked interpretation of a folding experiment
Suppose a hypothetical enzyme loses activity after urea exposure but recovers after careful removal of urea, while its peptide sequence remains unchanged. This is consistent with reversible disruption of a functional fold, not proof that peptide bonds were hydrolysed and resynthesised. If instead fragments appear after protease treatment, backbone cleavage is a plausible different explanation. Activity, sequence analysis and aggregation measurements supply complementary evidence.

## Short concept checks
- Is a two-domain single polypeptide automatically a dimer? **Answer:** No. Domains are regions within a chain; a dimer contains two subunits.
- Must ordinary denaturation destroy the primary peptide sequence? **Answer:** No. Folding interactions may be disrupted while backbone connectivity remains intact.

## Key takeaways
A fold depends on the chain and its environment. Separate domains, subunits, aggregation, unfolding and covalent cleavage when interpreting evidence.
