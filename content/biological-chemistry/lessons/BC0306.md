# Reading the periodic table from electronic structure

## Learning goals
Relate periods, groups and blocks to electronic structure. Translate older main-group notation without assigning an incorrect valence count to modern group numbers.

## Organisation and recurring chemistry
The modern periodic table orders elements by increasing atomic number. Mendeleev's historical organisation and predictions were foundational, but the modern atomic-number basis should not be attributed to a complete nineteenth-century knowledge of nuclei. Electronic structure explains many recurring chemical patterns as successive elements occupy available states.

A period is a horizontal row. A group is a vertical column. The modern table has seven periods and eighteen numbered groups. Older main-group conventions number eight principal groups; this is the convention behind some lecturer statements. It does not mean that the modern periodic table has only eight columns.

For main-group elements, similar outer configurations help explain similar chemistry within a group. Li, Na and K have an outer ns¹ pattern beyond a noble-gas core. Their different n values change size and other properties, so similar does not mean identical. Carbon and silicon belong to group 14 despite substantial chemical differences; describing them as “not a family” must not be interpreted as removing them from the same group.

Blocks describe the subshell type characteristic of the filling pattern: s, p, d or f. Main-group elements occupy the s and p blocks. The d region contains transition-series elements, though the strict definition of a transition element involves incomplete d subshells in the atom or a cation and does not simply include every displayed d-block element without qualification. The f series is usually displayed below the main body to keep the table compact; it belongs within periods 6 and 7.

## Worked group and block interpretation
Magnesium is [Ne] 3s². It is in period 3 and group 2, with two outer s electrons. Chlorine is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵: period 3, group 17, seven main-group valence electrons. For groups 13–18, the common main-group valence count is the group number minus ten, with helium as an important exception. Group 17 therefore does not mean seventeen valence electrons.

Helium is 1s². It has two electrons and s-type electronic structure, but is conventionally placed in group 18 because of its noble-gas chemistry. Table position and block rules need this stated exception rather than forcing helium to have p electrons.

Period 3 contains eight elements because its ground-state sequence covers 3s and 3p before 4s occupation begins. This is compatible with the n = 3 shell having capacity for 18 electrons when 3d is included. Period length and shell capacity answer different questions.

## Comparisons and limitations
“Same group” predicts useful broad similarities, not equal rates, identical biological roles or interchangeable binding. Sodium and potassium have related chemistry yet cells distinguish them through proteins and hydration-dependent interactions. A main-group shortcut should not be applied automatically to transition-metal valence or oxidation states.

## Short concept checks
- How many main-group valence electrons does chlorine in group 17 usually have? **Answer:** Seven, from 3s² 3p⁵; modern group number is not itself the electron count.
- Why is the detached f-block display not an extra pair of periods? **Answer:** It is a compact presentation of parts of periods 6 and 7.

## Key takeaways
Use atomic number for order, periods for rows, groups for recurring configurations and blocks for subshell patterns. State the numbering convention and exceptions before applying shortcuts.
