# Atomic models: evidence, explanations and limits

## Learning goals
Connect the Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr models to the observations they explain. Distinguish a successful prediction from a claim that a historical model describes every feature of an atom.

## How experiments changed the model
Dalton's atomic theory organised evidence that elements form compounds in characteristic proportions and that ordinary chemical reactions conserve mass. Treating matter as atoms made these regularities intelligible. Some original statements need qualification: atoms contain subatomic particles, isotopes of one element have different masses, and nuclear reactions can change element identity. These qualifications do not make the theory's chemical reasoning useless. In an ordinary balanced chemical reaction, atoms of each element are rearranged rather than created from nothing.

Cathode-ray experiments helped establish electrons as negatively charged constituents of atoms. Deflection by electric and magnetic fields supplied evidence about charged particles; the conclusion was not a direct photograph of a miniature solid ball. Thomson's model placed negative constituents within diffuse positive charge. It explained overall neutrality but could not account adequately for the later scattering evidence.

In the Rutherford scattering experiment, most positively charged alpha particles passed through a thin foil with little deflection, while a small proportion were strongly deflected or returned. A diffuse positive distribution could not readily produce those rare large deflections. A compact, massive, positively charged nucleus accounts for them. Most atomic volume lies outside that nucleus. Calling this volume “empty space” is a structural shorthand: electron probability and electromagnetic interactions still matter there, including in bonding and molecular encounters.

A classical electron moving continuously around the nucleus raises a stability problem. Bohr introduced permitted energy states for hydrogen. An electron changing between these states absorbs or emits a photon whose energy equals the difference. Discrete energy differences explain a line spectrum. The model is useful for hydrogen and hydrogen-like one-electron ions, but its fixed orbits do not provide a general modern description of many-electron atoms.

## Worked evidence chain
Imagine 10,000 alpha particles encounter a thin foil. Nearly all continue approximately forward, but a few turn sharply. The frequent observation constrains the distribution of matter over atomic volume; the rare observation constrains the concentration of positive charge and mass. Using only the frequent observation would miss the nucleus. Using only the rare observation could incorrectly suggest that the whole atom is a dense solid sphere.

For a hydrogen transition from a higher to a lower permitted energy, the atom loses energy and emits a photon. An upward transition requires absorption of the corresponding energy. A line identifies an energy difference, not a visible electron track. This same discipline—observation, model, prediction, limitation—will help evaluate biological diagrams later.

| Model | Central contribution | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Dalton | Atomic explanation of chemical proportions | Original indivisibility and identical-mass claims fail |
| Thomson | Electrons within an overall neutral atom | Diffuse positive charge fails large-angle scattering |
| Rutherford | Compact positive nucleus | Classical orbit picture does not resolve atomic stability |
| Bohr | Quantised hydrogen energy states | Fixed paths do not generalise to many-electron atoms |

## Misconceptions and scope
The lecturer's “Thompson” and “Neil Bohr” labels refer to J. J. Thomson and Niels Bohr. Correct spelling is recorded rather than assessed as a scientific objective. A historical diagram is a model, not a scale drawing. Rutherford evidence does not establish the later quantum-number rules by itself; quantum theory addresses a different level of explanation.

## Short concept checks
- Which scattering observation most strongly challenges diffuse positive charge? **Answer:** The rare very large deflections, which require a concentrated source of strong repulsion.
- Does an emitted spectral photon accompany an upward or downward atomic energy transition? **Answer:** Downward: the atom loses the energy carried by the photon.

## Key takeaways
A model earns its value by explaining evidence within a stated scope. Atomic composition, nuclear structure and quantised energies are distinct contributions; modern atomic theory retains useful insights while correcting earlier limitations.
