# Glycosidic notation, reducing ends and condensation arithmetic

## Learning goals
Read linkage notation, locate free anomeric centres and explain reducing behaviour. Calculate the bond and water counts for a specified connected carbohydrate structure.

## Read both carbon numbers and configuration
In Glcα(1→4)Glc, the first glucose's anomeric C1 is α and is linked through oxygen to C4 of the second residue. The notation does not mean α refers to the second residue's free C1. A linkage can therefore remain α(1→4) while the reducing residue changes between α and β anomers. Galβ(1→4)Glc identifies a different first sugar and linkage configuration.

Sucrose uses both anomeric carbons: glucose C1 and fructose C2. A fuller description is α-D-glucopyranosyl-(1↔2)-β-D-fructofuranoside. Including β on fructose matters; the source's abbreviated Glcα1→2Fru notation does not imply an unspecified free fructose anomeric OH. Carbon numbering and configuration are chemical information, not decorative labels.

## Find the reducing centre by chemistry
A free hemiacetal or hemiketal centre can open and expose carbonyl chemistry. Maltose and lactose retain a free glucose anomeric centre, so they are reducing disaccharides and can mutarotate there. In sucrose both anomeric centres are glycosidically locked, so the intact disaccharide is nonreducing under the standard tests discussed here.

Reducing means reducing another reagent while the sugar is oxidised; it does not mean the sugar contains fewer atoms or supplies fewer calories. Fructose gives positive alkaline reducing tests through enediol-mediated rearrangement despite being a ketose. Thus neither ketose nor disaccharide is by itself a sufficient negative-test label. Hydrolysing sucrose produces reducing monosaccharides and changes what the test can detect.

## Worked mass and water accounting
For ideal formation of a disaccharide from two free hexoses, add their formulas and subtract one water: 2 C₆H₁₂O₆ − H₂O = C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. With illustrative molar masses 180 g/mol per hexose and 18 g/mol water, the product is 342 g/mol. This is stoichiometric accounting, not a claim that simply mixing two sugars makes the glycosidic bond in water without activation.

A connected, acyclic chain or branched tree containing n monosaccharide units has n−1 linking bonds. Forming those ideal glycosidic links from free units corresponds to losing n−1 waters. Five glucose units give four bonds and formula C₃₀H₅₂O₂₆: 5×C₆H₁₂O₆−4H₂O. Branching changes the topology but not that bond count for a connected tree. A cycle or cross-linked network needs its actual extra bond count; do not apply n−1 blindly.

## Short concept checks
- Does maltose's α(1→4) linkage mean its reducing glucose must always have an α free OH? **Answer:** No. The α descriptor specifies the glycosidic donor; the free reducing centre can mutarotate independently.
- How many ideal linking waters correspond to a connected acyclic six-unit glycan? **Answer:** Five, one per glycosidic link under the stated free-unit condensation accounting.

## Key takeaways
Identify every anomeric carbon before predicting reduction or mutarotation. Specify the topology and chemical assumptions before using formula arithmetic.
